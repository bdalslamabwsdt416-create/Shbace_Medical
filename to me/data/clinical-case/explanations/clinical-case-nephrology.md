## CC-0020 — Iron deficiency & childhood anemia

**Question:** A 1 year old infant is complaining of delayed sitting and repeated chest infection, on
examination there is prominent costochondral junction, he is exclusively breast fed,
He received multiple injections for treatment of his condition. All of the following
are expected complication for his condition except:
1. Anorexia
2. Vomiting
3. Oliguria
4. Nephrocalcinosis

**Answer status:** No verified selectable answer is encoded; no answer has been guessed.

Clinical explanation:
Excess vitamin D can cause hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria, presenting with anorexia, vomiting, polyuria, dehydration and nephrocalcinosis. Oliguria is not the typical early expected feature; severe renal injury may later reduce urine output.

Options analysis:
1 and 2: common hypercalcaemia symptoms. 3: intended “except” answer, though severe renal injury can eventually cause oliguria. 4: recognized complication of hypercalciuria.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS Growth and Nutrition/Nephrology; vitamin D toxicity and renal complications.

High-Yield:
Vitamin D toxicity causes hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria; monitor calcium and renal function.

## CC-0041 — Urinary tract infection

**Question:** A 4 month old infant presents with fever and poor feeding. Examination revealed
moderate dehydration, poor perfusion, and screaming. The WBCs count is elevated
with shift to the left. Urine analysis of a catheterized specimen reveals red blood
cells, white blood cells, and scant bacteria. What is the most appropriate course of
treatment?

**Options:**
- A. Fluid restriction.
- B. Surgical intervention
- C. I.V. antibiotics therapy
- D. Wait for culture results

**Answer:** C — I.V. antibiotics therapy

Clinical explanation:
A febrile infant with poor perfusion, dehydration and inflammatory urine from a catheterized specimen has a likely complicated UTI/urosepsis. Start prompt parenteral antibiotics after cultures if this does not delay treatment, and manage perfusion.

Options analysis:
A: fluid restriction worsens dehydration. B: surgery is not indicated from the data. C: correct. D: do not wait for culture results before treating a sick infant.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Nephrology; UTI, urine testing and complicated infection.

High-Yield:
A catheterized sample is useful for culture in infants; do not delay sepsis treatment.

- A4 merged MD; original source: `problem_solving-with-answers.pdf, source p. 17`.

## CC-0043 — Chronic kidney disease & renal function

**Question:** An 8 year old boy comes complaining of bedwetting for the past 2 weeks. He has
previously been continent. On examination, his height is below 5th percentile. His
Hb is 6.5% what is the most important next step

**Options:**
- A. Check blood sugar
- B. Give oral iron
- C. Try fluid restriction and rewarding for dry nights
- D. Check BUN and creatinine

**Answer:** D — Check BUN and creatinine

Clinical explanation:
Previously continent child with new enuresis, short stature and severe anemia requires evaluation for chronic kidney disease, among other causes.

Options analysis:
A: check glucose if polyuria/polydipsia is present, but the growth and anemia clues point to renal disease. B: oral iron alone misses possible CKD. C: behavioural management is for primary monosymptomatic enuresis, not a child with systemic red flags. D: correct.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Nephrology; AKI/CKD and renal manifestations.

High-Yield:
New secondary enuresis with growth failure or anaemia is a medical red flag, not simple bedwetting.

## CC-0044 — Urinary tract infection

**Question:** A 4 year old boy, whose past medical history is positive for 3 UTI. now presents with
BP 135/90, renal scan shows bilateral renal scars. What should have been done to
prevent this situation:

**Options:**
- A. Give antibiotics for 3-5 days for each UTI
- B. Do cystourethrography and give prophylactic antibiotics accordingly
- C. Abd U/S every 3 months
- D. Prescribe urinary effervescent

**Answer:** B — Do cystourethrography and give prophylactic antibiotics accordingly

Clinical explanation:
Recurrent UTIs with renal scarring suggest an underlying predisposition such as VUR. Imaging and prevention are needed to reduce recurrent pyelonephritis and scarring.

Options analysis:
A: short courses alone do not investigate the cause. B: correct in the source option set, though imaging/prophylaxis should follow current age- and risk-based guidance. C: routine ultrasound every 3 months does not diagnose reflux. D: urinary effervescent preparations do not prevent renal scarring.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Nephrology; recurrent UTI, VUR and renal scarring.

High-Yield:
A normal renal ultrasound does not exclude VUR; imaging decisions depend on age and recurrence/atypical features.

## CC-0082 — Urinary tract infection

**Question:** A 4 year old boy, whose past medical history is positive for 3 UTI.now
presents with BP 135/90, renal scan, shows bilateral renal scars.
What should have been done to prevent this situation?

**Options:**
- A. give antibiotics for 3-5 days for each UTI
- B. do cystourethrography and give prophylactic Abs accordingly
- C. abdominal U/S every 3 months
- D. prescribe urinary effervescent

**Answer:** B — do cystourethrography and give prophylactic Abs accordingly

Clinical explanation:
Recurrent UTIs, bilateral renal scars and hypertension suggest reflux nephropathy. VCUG can identify vesicoureteral reflux; prevention requires prompt diagnosis and treatment of UTIs and selected reflux-directed strategies. Antibiotic prophylaxis is not automatic for every child and depends on reflux grade and clinical context.

Source explanation and reasoning:
Explanation of the case: 
            - Renal scars by scan indicate fibrosis from repeated inflammations. (pyelonephritis) 
This caused the high blood pressure. 
 
⇒ Choice (2): is the right answer 
Points with: this is an investigation where a dye is injected through urethera, it is 
done to detect reflux at the level of the ureters when joining the 
bladder. This occurs in a congenital disease where when the bladder 
contracts to evacuate, the ureters are not completely compressed 
allowing refulx of urine.The reflux causes repeated infections. 
So detection of this disease and surgical correction was essential to 
prevent this condition.

Options analysis:
A — short courses may be inadequate for febrile pyelonephritis and do not assess reflux; B — correct as the best option, but prophylaxis/surgery should be individualized; C — ultrasound surveillance alone does not diagnose reflux; D — urinary effervescent has no established preventive role.
