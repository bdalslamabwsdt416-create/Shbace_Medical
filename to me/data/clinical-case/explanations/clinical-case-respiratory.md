## CC-0047 — Retropharyngeal abscess & upper-airway obstruction

**Question:** A 3 years old fully immunized child presents with fever and difficulty in breathing.
She has had tonsillitis over the past week, for which she received oral antibiotics for
2 days. On examination, she looks unwell, she has mild recession, and a soft
inspiratory sound is audible. What is the most likely diagnosis:

**Options:**
- A. Bronchial asthma
- B. Retro-pharyngeal abscess
- C. Epiglottitis
- D. Pneumonia

**Answer:** B — Retro-pharyngeal abscess

Clinical explanation:
Fever, ill appearance, respiratory difficulty and inspiratory noise after an inadequately treated throat infection suggest an upper-airway deep neck infection. A child with possible airway obstruction needs urgent airway-capable assessment.

Options analysis:
A: asthma usually causes expiratory wheeze. B: compatible with upper-airway obstruction after pharyngeal infection. C: epiglottitis remains an important differential, especially with drooling/tripod posture; the stem does not clearly exclude it. D: pneumonia causes lower respiratory findings, not stridor.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Respiratory/Infectious; upper-airway obstruction and acute respiratory emergencies.

High-Yield:
Stridor is an upper-airway warning sign; prioritize airway safety over routine investigations.

## CC-0048 — Pneumothorax & air leak

**Question:** A 2 month old boy with a 3 day history of mild fever and runny nose suddenly
develops high fever, cough and respiratory distress. Within 48 hours, the patient
deteriorated and has developed a peumatocele and a left sided pneumothorax. What is
the most appropriate first action?

**Options:**
- A. I.V. antibiotics
- B. Blood gases
- C. Chest tube
- D. Antipyretics

**Answer:** C — Chest tube

Clinical explanation:
A left pneumothorax with rapid respiratory deterioration is an emergency. Decompression/drainage takes priority over non-urgent testing or antipyretics.

Options analysis:
A: IV antibiotics treat the underlying infection but do not immediately relieve tension physiology. B: blood gases do not treat the pneumothorax. C: correct in the stated option set. D: antipyretics are not the immediate lifesaving intervention.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Respiratory; pneumothorax and emergency management.

High-Yield:
Treat the immediate threat first; do not delay decompression for imaging in an unstable tension pneumothorax.

## CC-0049 — Tuberculosis & chronic cough

**Question:** An 11 years old child complains of swollen glands in his neck and groin for the past 6
months and increasing cough over the previous 2 weeks. He also reports some fevers,
especially at night, and some weight loss .On examination , he has non tender cervical ,
axillary and inguinal nodes, no hepatosplenomegaly , and other systems are within
normal. What is the most appropriate next step ?

**Options:**
- A. Biopsy of anode .
- B. Complete blood count and differential.
- C. Trial of antituberculous drugs.
- D. Chest X-ray.

**Answer:** D — Chest X-ray.

Clinical explanation:
Chronic non-tender lymphadenopathy, night fever, weight loss and cough raise concern for tuberculosis and other chronic infections/malignancy. Chest radiography is a reasonable initial investigation; the source explanation’s claim that lymphoma is excluded by absent hepatosplenomegaly is too strong.

Options analysis:
A: node biopsy may be required if malignancy remains suspected or diagnosis is unresolved, but it is invasive. B: CBC is useful but not the best first targeted study here. C: do not start empiric anti-TB therapy without appropriate evaluation. D: correct among the listed options.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Respiratory/Infectious; childhood TB and chronic cough.

High-Yield:
Persistent lymphadenopathy with constitutional symptoms needs structured TB and malignancy evaluation.

## CC-0050 — Bronchiolitis & viral wheeze

**Question:** An 8 month old infant presents to the pediatrician with a 3 day history of nasal
discharge, temperature of a 38.5 C and decreased appetite. Examination reveals a
tachypneic infant with audible wheezing. There is flaring of alae nasi as well as
intercostals and subcostal retractions .What is the most likely diagnosis ?

**Options:**
- A. Croup.( this is one of the causes of stridor due to viral infection of the air ways as in case of acute laryngitis or tracheobronchitis).
- B. Acute bronchiolitis.
- C. Acute asthma.
- D. Acute epiglottitis

**Answer:** B — Acute bronchiolitis.

Clinical explanation:
An 8-month-old with coryza followed by wheeze, tachypnoea, nasal flaring and intercostal/subcostal recession fits bronchiolitis.

Options analysis:
A: croup usually causes barking cough and inspiratory stridor. B: correct. C: first asthma-like episode at this age with viral prodrome is more consistent with bronchiolitis. D: epiglottitis usually causes toxic appearance, drooling and upper-airway obstruction.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Respiratory; bronchiolitis and infant wheeze.

High-Yield:
Bronchiolitis is common in infants and usually follows a viral upper respiratory prodrome.

## CC-0051 — Bronchiolitis & viral wheeze

**Question:** A previously well 1 year old infant has had a runny nose and has been sneezing and
coughing for 2 days. Two other members of the family had similar symptoms. Four
hours ago, his cough became much worse. On physical examination, he is in moderate
respiratory distress with nasal flaring, hyperexpansion of the chest and easily audible
wheezing without rales (crepitations) by auscultation. Which of the following is the
most likely diagnosis?

**Options:**
- A. Bronchilitis
- B. Viral croup
- C. Asthma
- D. Epiglottitis

**Answer:** A — Bronchilitis

Clinical explanation:
A 1-year-old with household viral URI exposure, wheezing and hyperinflation without focal crackles is consistent with bronchiolitis.

Options analysis:
A: correct. B: croup causes barking cough/stridor. C: asthma may cause wheeze but age and first viral episode make bronchiolitis more likely. D: epiglottitis causes upper-airway signs and toxicity.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Respiratory; bronchiolitis.

High-Yield:
Wheeze in an infant after a viral prodrome is most often bronchiolitis.

## CC-0052 — Pneumonia complicated by sepsis

**Question:** A 1 year previously healthy male was admitted to the hospital in the last evening, he
presented with cough, high fever, and mild hypoxia. CXR revealed right upper lobar
consolidation. Approximately 20 hours after hospitalization. the patient deteriorates;
developing severe respiratory distress, hypotension, poor perfusion and his color
becomesdusky.What is the most likely diagnosis?

**Options:**
- A. Uncomplicated pneumonia
- B. Pneumonia complicated by sepsis
- C. Pneumonia complicated by heart failure
- D. Pneumonia complicated by pneumothorax

**Answer:** B — Pneumonia complicated by sepsis

Clinical explanation:
Deterioration after lobar pneumonia with hypotension, poor perfusion and dusky colour indicates systemic shock/sepsis.

Options analysis:
A: uncomplicated pneumonia does not explain shock. B: correct. C: heart failure is not the best fit without supporting signs. D: pneumothorax would usually have asymmetric breath sounds/acute pleural signs.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Respiratory/Infectious; pneumonia, sepsis and shock.

High-Yield:
Hypotension and poor perfusion in a child with pneumonia indicate severe disease and require urgent resuscitation.

## CC-0053 — Respiratory syncytial virus & bronchiolitis

**Question:** Patient 1 year old having rhinorrhea and sneezing for 4 days then developed cough
difficulty in breathing examination revealed moderate distress and x- ray shows
hyperinflated chest the most likely causative organism is:

**Options:**
- A. Staph aureeus
- B. Haemophilus influenza
- C. Echo virus
- D. Respiratory syncitial virus

**Answer:** D — Respiratory syncitial virus

Clinical explanation:
A 1-year-old with coryza followed by cough, respiratory distress and hyperinflation has a typical bronchiolitis presentation; RSV is the commonest classic cause.

Options analysis:
A: Staphylococcus aureus is associated with severe necrotizing pneumonia, pneumatoceles and empyema. B: Hib is less likely in an immunized child and does not classically cause bronchiolitis. C: echovirus is not the usual cause. D: correct.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Respiratory; bronchiolitis and common viral pathogens.

High-Yield:
RSV is a leading cause of bronchiolitis in infants.

## CC-0089 — Pneumothorax & air leak

**Question:** A 2-month-old boy with a 3 day history of mild fever and runny nose
suddenly develops high fever, cough and respiratory distress. Within
48 hours, the patient deteriorated and has developed a peumatocele
and a left sided pneumothorax.
What is the most appropriate first action?

**Options:**
- A. I.V. antibiotics
- B. Blood gases
- C. Chest tube
- D. Antipyretics

**Answer:** C — Chest tube

Clinical explanation:
A pneumatocele with a new pneumothorax and respiratory distress after severe pneumonia is an emergency. Chest drainage is the intended first action among the choices; if tension pneumothorax is clinically suspected, immediate needle/finger decompression precedes tube thoracostomy. Antibiotics are also required but do not replace urgent pleural decompression.
