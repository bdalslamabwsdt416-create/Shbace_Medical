## CC-0021 — Sickle cell disease & complications

**Question:** A 14 years old boy has sickle cell disease. He presents to the emergency room with
increased jaundice, pain in the right upper quadrant with guarding, and a clear
chest. CXR is normal. Which of the following tests is more likely to reveal the cause
of pain:

**Options:**
- A. Serum chemistries
- B. CBC with platelets, DD
- C. Ultrasound abdomen ( to exclude pigmentary gall stones)
- D. Upper GIT endoscopy

**Answer:** C — Ultrasound abdomen ( to exclude pigmentary gall stones)

Clinical explanation:
In sickle cell disease, right-upper-quadrant pain and jaundice raise concern for pigment gallstones or biliary disease. Ultrasound is the preferred initial imaging test for gallstones.

Options analysis:
A: chemistry tests may show abnormalities but do not identify stones. B: CBC/D-dimer is not the best test to detect gallstones. C: correct. D: endoscopy does not assess gallbladder stones.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Hematology/GIT; sickle cell disease and pigment gallstones.

High-Yield:
Chronic haemolysis increases unconjugated bilirubin and pigment gallstone risk.

## CC-0022 — Iron deficiency & childhood anemia

**Question:** On a routine well child examination, a 1 year old boy is noted to be pale. He is in
seventy-fifth percentile for weight and twenty-fifth percentile for length. Results of
physical examination are other normal. His hematocrit is 24%. The answer to which
of the following questions is most likely to be helpful in making diagnosis?

**Options:**
- A. what is the child's usual daily diet?
- B. did the child receive phototherapy for neonatal jaundice?
- C. has anyone in the family received a blood transfusion?
- D. what is the pattern and appearance of his bowel movements?

**Answer:** A — what is the child's usual daily diet?

Clinical explanation:
A pale 1-year-old with low haematocrit and no other findings requires a nutritional history; iron deficiency is common and dietary iron intake is a key determinant.

Options analysis:
A: correct. B: neonatal phototherapy is not the first dietary clue. C: transfusion history is less relevant without haemolysis clues. D: bowel pattern may help in selected cases but is not the most useful first question.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Hematology/Growth and Nutrition; iron deficiency anemia.

High-Yield:
Dietary history is a first-line part of evaluating childhood microcytic anemia.

- A4 merged MD; original source: `problem_solving-with-answers.pdf, source p. 13`.

## CC-0024 — Iron deficiency & childhood anemia

**Question:** A 10 month old female infant is brought to a clinic for routine health evaluation.
Her diet consists of ordinary food and a lot of fresh whole milk. On examination, she
is pale, hemoglobin is 7.5 gm% otherwise there are no abnormalities. The most
likely diagnosis:

**Options:**
- A. Thalassemia
- B. Iron deficiency anemia
- C. Sickle cell anemia
- D. Anemia of chronic illness

**Answer:** B — Iron deficiency anemia

Clinical explanation:
A 10-month-old consuming large amounts of cow’s milk and with Hb 7.5 g/dL is at high risk of iron deficiency due to low iron intake and displacement of iron-rich foods.

Options analysis:
A: thalassemia usually has microcytosis with a relatively high RBC count and often family/ethnic clues. B: correct. C: sickle cell disease usually has additional haemolytic/clinical findings. D: no chronic inflammatory illness is described.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Hematology/Growth; iron deficiency and childhood anemia.

High-Yield:
Excess cow’s milk intake is a common nutritional risk for iron deficiency in toddlers.

- A4 merged MD; original source: `problem_solving-with-answers.pdf, source p. 13`.

## CC-0025 — G6PD deficiency & haemolysis

**Question:** A 4-year-old previously well boy is brought to clinic because he developed pallor/
dark urine, and jaundice over the past few days. He is taking trimethoprim-
sulfamethoxazole for olitis media. Laboratory testing shows low hemoglobin and
hematocrit and an elevated bilirubin level. .What is the most likely diagnosis?

**Options:**
- A. Hepatitis A .
- B. Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency
- C. Drug-induced hepatitis
- D. Galactosemia

**Answer:** B — Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency

Clinical explanation:
Acute pallor, dark urine and jaundice after trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole suggest oxidant-triggered haemolysis in G6PD deficiency.

Options analysis:
A: hepatitis does not explain acute haemolytic anaemia. B: correct. C: drug-induced hepatitis would produce a different liver profile and does not explain haemolysis as well. D: galactosaemia presents much earlier with feeding intolerance/liver dysfunction.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Hematology; G6PD deficiency and haemolysis.

High-Yield:
G6PD testing can be falsely normal during acute haemolysis because older deficient cells have been destroyed; repeat after recovery if suspicion remains.
