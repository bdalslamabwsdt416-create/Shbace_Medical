## EB-paediatrics-preventive-and-social-medicine-accepted-Q17 — Q17 — Preventive medicine / neonatal health

**Source:** Question-bank MD p.151 · printed p.283 · answer-key PDF p.153 · original Q17

**Initial explanation**

Neonatal tetanus is vaccine-preventable through maternal tetanus immunization and clean delivery/cord-care practices. Maternal antibodies protect the newborn during the early neonatal period. The question is asking for a preventable cause of neonatal death, not claiming tetanus is the only preventable cause.

**Detailed explanation**

Neonatal tetanus is vaccine-preventable through maternal tetanus immunization and clean delivery/cord-care practices. Maternal antibodies protect the newborn during the early neonatal period. The question is asking for a preventable cause of neonatal death, not claiming tetanus is the only preventable cause. **Why the others are wrong:** (a) Tetanus: correct among these options because maternal tetanus vaccination can prevent neonatal tetanus. (b) Septicaemia: neonatal sepsis can be reduced by prevention and appropriate care, but this option does not refer to a routinely vaccine-preventable neonatal cause in the way tetanus does. (c) Necrotising enterocolitis: no routine vaccine prevents it. (d) RDS: not prevented by a routine vaccine.

Curriculum alignment: Neonatology 2024–2025, pp.3–4: neonatal mortality definition; Pediatric Vaccination, pp.2–3 and p.11: tetanus toxoid and EPI targets.

High-yield point: Maternal tetanus-containing vaccine, clean birth practices, and clean cord care work together; vaccination alone is not the whole prevention strategy.

**Initial explanation**

A nosocomial infection is an infection acquired in a healthcare setting that was not present or incubating at the time of admission. The term is commonly used for hospital-acquired infections; onset timing alone is not always sufficient to prove acquisition in hospital.

**Detailed explanation**

A nosocomial infection is an infection acquired in a healthcare setting that was not present or incubating at the time of admission. The term is commonly used for hospital-acquired infections; onset timing alone is not always sufficient to prove acquisition in hospital. **Why the others are wrong:** (a) Hospital-acquired infection: correct definition. (b) Epidemic over a wider area: describes an epidemic, not nosocomial infection. (c) Community-acquired infection: acquired outside the healthcare facility. (d) None: incorrect.

Incidence measures the occurrence of new cases in a population at risk over a specified time interval. In formal epidemiology, an incidence proportion (risk) uses the number of new cases over the period divided by the population at risk at the start; an incidence rate uses person-time. The option is a simplified definition of new-case frequency.

**Detailed explanation**

Incidence measures the occurrence of new cases in a population at risk over a specified time interval. In formal epidemiology, an incidence proportion (risk) uses the number of new cases over the period divided by the population at risk at the start; an incidence rate uses person-time. ## EB-paediatrics-preventive-and-social-medicine-accepted-Q73 — Q73 — Epidemiology / measure of disease frequency

**Source:** Question-bank MD p.152 · printed p.284 · answer-key PDF p.153 · original Q73

**Initial explanation**

Prevalence describes the proportion or number of people in a defined population who have a condition at a specified point or during a specified period. It includes existing cases (both previously diagnosed and newly occurring cases that are present during the reference window). The wording “old and new cases in a specific period” is an approximation of period prevalence.

**Detailed explanation**

Prevalence describes the proportion or number of people in a defined population who have a condition at a specified point or during a specified period. It includes existing cases (both previously diagnosed and newly occurring cases that are present during the reference window). The wording “old and new cases in a specific period” is an approximation of period prevalence. **Why the others are wrong:** (a) New cases during a period: incidence, not prevalence. (b) Number of cases in a defined population: incomplete because it omits the reference time and denominator. (c) Old and new cases in a defined population during a specified period: best available option for period prevalence. (d) All of the above: incorrect because a describes incidence.

Curriculum alignment: Curriculum match: no explicit incidence/prevalence definition was found in the supplied 19 course sources. This is foundational epidemiology rather than a clearly indexed pediatric lecture topic.

High-yield point: Distinguish point prevalence (at one moment) from period prevalence (at any time during a defined period).
