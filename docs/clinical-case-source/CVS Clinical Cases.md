## Clinical Case 1 — A symptomatic infant with a congenital shunt

A 6-week-old infant is brought with poor feeding, sweating during feeds and poor weight gain. Examination shows tachypnoea, tachycardia, a pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border and mild hepatomegaly. The infant has signs of pulmonary overcirculation and early heart failure.

### Q1. [Easy] What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Ventricular septal defect  
B. Coarctation of the aorta  
C. Atrial septal defect  
D. Patent ductus arteriosus  
E. Tetralogy of Fallot  

**Correct Answer:** A. Ventricular septal defect

**Initial Explanation:**

The echocardiogram confirms a defect in the interventricular septum. The clinical picture is also typical of a large VSD causing a left-to-right shunt, pulmonary overcirculation and heart failure during early infancy.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Ventricular septal defect — Correct.** A VSD is a communication between the ventricles. A large defect can cause pulmonary overcirculation, heart failure, recurrent chest infections and failure to thrive.
- **B. Coarctation of the aorta — Incorrect.** Coarctation produces upper–lower limb blood-pressure and pulse discrepancies; a critically affected neonate may present with circulatory collapse when the ductus closes.
- **C. Atrial septal defect — Incorrect.** ASD is an interatrial communication. Its characteristic auscultatory finding is a fixed wide split second heart sound, often with an ejection systolic flow murmur at the upper left sternal border.
- **D. Patent ductus arteriosus — Incorrect.** PDA typically produces a continuous machinery murmur in the left infraclavicular area and may cause bounding pulses and a wide pulse pressure when large.
- **E. Tetralogy of Fallot — Incorrect.** TOF is a cyanotic lesion associated with pulmonary outflow obstruction, a VSD, overriding aorta and right ventricular hypertrophy.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- VSD is the most common congenital heart defect in the supplied sheet.
- A large VSD usually produces a left-to-right shunt and increased pulmonary blood flow.
- Feeding difficulty, sweating, tachypnoea, hepatomegaly and poor growth suggest heart failure in an infant.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Pages 8–10.

# 0. Introductory / Unsectioned Questions

### Q2. [Easy] Which haemodynamic change is primarily responsible for the infant's pulmonary congestion?

A. Left-to-right shunting with increased pulmonary blood flow  
B. Reduced blood flow from the left ventricle into the aorta  
C. Increased systemic vascular resistance from coarctation  
D. Complete obstruction of pulmonary venous return  
E. Right-to-left shunting with reduced pulmonary blood flow  

**Correct Answer:** A. Left-to-right shunting with increased pulmonary blood flow

**Initial Explanation:**

After birth, left ventricular pressure is higher than right ventricular pressure. Blood therefore passes through the VSD from left to right, increasing pulmonary blood flow. A large shunt can cause pulmonary congestion and heart failure.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Left-to-right shunting — Correct.** Oxygenated blood recirculates through the pulmonary circulation, increasing pulmonary blood flow and cardiac workload.
- **B. Reduced aortic flow — Incorrect.** The central problem in a large uncomplicated VSD is excessive pulmonary flow, not primary obstruction of systemic outflow.
- **C. Increased systemic vascular resistance — Incorrect.** Coarctation can increase left ventricular afterload, but it does not explain the VSD-related pulmonary overcirculation in this case.
- **D. Pulmonary venous obstruction — Incorrect.** This is not the mechanism of an isolated VSD.
- **E. Right-to-left shunting — Incorrect.** This causes systemic desaturation and cyanosis, as may occur in advanced pulmonary vascular disease with Eisenmenger physiology or in cyanotic congenital heart disease.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Large VSD → left-to-right shunt → increased pulmonary blood flow → pulmonary congestion and heart failure.
- Symptoms often become more apparent after pulmonary vascular resistance falls following birth.
- Persistent large shunts can cause pulmonary vascular disease and eventual shunt reversal.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Pages 8–11.

### Q3. [Moderate] Which explanation best accounts for the fact that the murmur is not particularly loud despite the large defect and significant symptoms?

A. Heart failure prevents all turbulent blood flow  
B. Large defects always produce low pulmonary blood flow  
C. The murmur is quiet because the aortic valve is stenosed  
D. The pressure difference between the ventricles may be smaller across a large non-restrictive defect  
E. The defect must have closed spontaneously  

**Correct Answer:** D. The pressure difference between the ventricles may be smaller across a large non-restrictive defect

**Initial Explanation:**

A small restrictive VSD may generate a high-velocity jet and a loud murmur. In a large non-restrictive VSD, ventricular pressures become more similar, reducing the pressure gradient and potentially producing a softer murmur.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Heart failure does not abolish turbulence. The more relevant explanation is the smaller pressure gradient across a large defect.
- **B. Incorrect.** A large VSD commonly increases pulmonary blood flow unless pulmonary vascular resistance becomes markedly elevated.
- **C. Incorrect.** Aortic stenosis is not the explanation for the murmur intensity described in the VSD section.
- **D. Correct.** The ventricles communicate freely, so the pressure gradient across the defect may be relatively small. Murmur intensity therefore does not reliably reflect disease severity.
- **E. Incorrect.** A defect that has closed would not explain the echocardiographic finding or the current haemodynamic symptoms.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Small restrictive VSD: often a loud pansystolic murmur.
- Large non-restrictive VSD: murmur may be softer or occasionally difficult to hear.
- Never judge the severity of a VSD from murmur loudness alone; assess symptoms, growth, examination and echocardiography.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Pages 8–9.

### Q4. [Moderate] Which investigation is the definitive diagnostic test and can assess the defect's anatomy and pulmonary pressure?

A. Twelve-lead ECG  
B. Echocardiography  
C. Chest X-ray  
D. Pulse oximetry alone  
E. Full blood count  

**Correct Answer:** B. Echocardiography

**Initial Explanation:**

Echocardiography is the diagnostic investigation for VSD. It defines the anatomy and helps assess the haemodynamic consequences, including pulmonary hypertension.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. ECG — Incorrect.** It may show ventricular hypertrophy, including biventricular hypertrophy in a significant VSD, but it is not the definitive anatomical test.
- **B. Echocardiography — Correct.** It demonstrates the defect, its location and haemodynamic effects.
- **C. Chest X-ray — Incorrect.** It may show cardiomegaly, pulmonary plethora or pulmonary oedema in a large VSD, but it does not define the septal defect.
- **D. Pulse oximetry — Incorrect.** It assesses oxygen saturation and may be normal in an uncomplicated left-to-right shunt; it does not show the defect.
- **E. Full blood count — Incorrect.** It may help assess associated problems but cannot establish the structural diagnosis.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- CXR in a significant VSD: cardiomegaly and pulmonary plethora; pulmonary oedema may be present.
- ECG may show biventricular hypertrophy.
- Echocardiography is diagnostic and assesses anatomy and pulmonary pressure.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Page 9.

### Q5. [Moderate] Which combination is most consistent with a haemodynamically significant large VSD?

A. Normal growth, normal chest X-ray and no signs of heart failure  
B. Boot-shaped heart with oligemic lung fields  
C. Egg-on-side heart with increased pulmonary vascular markings  
D. Cardiomegaly, pulmonary plethora and evidence of ventricular hypertrophy  
E. Rib notching with isolated upper-limb hypertension  

**Correct Answer:** D. Cardiomegaly, pulmonary plethora and evidence of ventricular hypertrophy

**Initial Explanation:**

A large left-to-right shunt increases pulmonary blood flow and cardiac workload. The sheet describes cardiomegaly and pulmonary plethora on chest X-ray, with biventricular hypertrophy on ECG.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** This is more compatible with a small VSD without significant haemodynamic consequences.
- **B. Incorrect.** A boot-shaped heart with oligemic lung fields is classically associated with TOF.
- **C. Incorrect.** An egg-on-side appearance with increased pulmonary vascular markings is associated with TGA in the sheet.
- **D. Correct.** Cardiomegaly, pulmonary plethora and ventricular hypertrophy fit a large VSD with pulmonary overcirculation.
- **E. Incorrect.** Rib notching and upper-limb hypertension suggest coarctation rather than VSD.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Large VSD: CXR — cardiomegaly and plethoric lungs.
- ECG — biventricular hypertrophy may occur.
- Small VSD may have a normal CXR and ECG.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Pages 9 and 16.

### Q6. [Moderate] What is the appropriate general management approach for this symptomatic infant while definitive correction is being planned?

A. Treat heart failure, support nutrition and arrange early defect assessment for closure  
B. Wait until adulthood even if heart failure persists  
C. Restrict all feeding to prevent tachypnoea  
D. Reassurance only and no follow-up  
E. Give pulmonary vasodilators as the sole treatment for every VSD  

**Correct Answer:** A. Treat heart failure, support nutrition and arrange early defect assessment for closure

**Initial Explanation:**

The sheet describes treatment of heart failure with decongestive therapy, including a diuretic and an ACE inhibitor, alongside nutritional support and early correction of a significant defect to prevent pulmonary hypertension, heart failure and faltering growth.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Correct.** Stabilize and treat heart failure, optimize feeding and growth, and assess for early surgical or catheter-based correction as appropriate to the anatomy.
- **B. Incorrect.** Delaying treatment of a haemodynamically important VSD risks progressive pulmonary vascular disease and other complications.
- **C. Incorrect.** Feeding support is important. Infants with tachypnoea may need an adapted feeding plan or enteral support, rather than simply having feeds withheld.
- **D. Incorrect.** Reassurance and observation may be suitable for selected small defects, not a symptomatic infant with heart failure and poor growth.
- **E. Incorrect.** Pulmonary vasodilators are not the sole routine treatment of an uncomplicated large VSD. The underlying shunt and heart failure must be addressed.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Treat heart failure and optimize nutrition.
- The sheet mentions diuretic therapy and captopril for heart failure.
- Early correction of a large VSD is intended to prevent pulmonary hypertension, heart failure and failure to thrive.
- Exact timing and method of closure depend on the defect and the child's clinical state.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Page 9.

### Q7. [Hard] A child with a previously large left-to-right VSD later develops cyanosis, clubbing, exertional dyspnoea and a loud pulmonary component of the second heart sound. Which complication best explains this change?

A. Acute bacterial endocarditis in every case  
B. Isolated mitral stenosis  
C. Eisenmenger syndrome due to pulmonary vascular disease and shunt reversal  
D. Physiological pulmonary flow murmur  
E. Spontaneous closure of the VSD  

**Correct Answer:** C. Eisenmenger syndrome due to pulmonary vascular disease and shunt reversal

**Initial Explanation:**

A large left-to-right shunt can cause progressive pulmonary vascular disease. Increased pulmonary vascular resistance may eventually reverse the shunt to right-to-left, producing cyanosis and other features of Eisenmenger syndrome.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Infective endocarditis is a recognized complication of some congenital lesions, but it does not explain this characteristic chronic sequence from a large shunt to pulmonary vascular disease and reversal.
- **B. Incorrect.** Mitral stenosis does not explain the history of a large VSD followed by shunt reversal.
- **C. Correct.** Shunt reversal due to elevated pulmonary vascular resistance explains the development of cyanosis and chronic hypoxia. The CVS sheet also describes a loud P2 and right ventricular hypertrophy.
- **D. Incorrect.** An innocent flow murmur is not associated with cyanosis, clubbing or pulmonary vascular disease.
- **E. Incorrect.** Spontaneous closure would not explain new cyanosis, clubbing and pulmonary hypertension.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Large unrepaired left-to-right shunt → pulmonary vascular disease → increased pulmonary vascular resistance → right-to-left shunt.
- Eisenmenger features in the sheet include cyanosis, dyspnoea, chest pain, haemoptysis, clubbing, loud P2 and RVH.
- Prevention depends on timely recognition and correction of significant shunts before irreversible pulmonary vascular disease develops.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Pages 9–11.

### Q8. [Hard] Which statement about a small VSD is most consistent with the sheet?

A. It always causes Eisenmenger syndrome before school age  
B. It requires immediate surgical repair in every child  
C. A normal echocardiogram is expected despite a persistent defect  
D. It invariably causes severe heart failure during the neonatal period  
E. It commonly closes spontaneously during the first few years, particularly when muscular  

**Correct Answer:** E. It commonly closes spontaneously during the first few years, particularly when muscular

**Initial Explanation:**

The sheet states that small VSDs, especially muscular defects, may close spontaneously during the first two to three years. Clinical and echocardiographic follow-up is required.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Eisenmenger syndrome is associated with significant longstanding shunts and pulmonary vascular disease, not the expected course of every small VSD.
- **B. Incorrect.** A small uncomplicated VSD is generally followed rather than automatically repaired.
- **C. Incorrect.** Echocardiography can identify a persistent small defect even when the child is asymptomatic.
- **D. Incorrect.** Small restrictive VSDs are often asymptomatic and do not usually cause severe heart failure.
- **E. Correct.** Spontaneous closure during early childhood is described, particularly for muscular VSDs.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Small VSD: often asymptomatic, with normal growth and no heart failure.
- Muscular VSDs are particularly likely to close spontaneously.
- The sheet recommends clinical and echocardiographic follow-up.
- Infective endocarditis risk and prophylaxis advice must follow the specific risk category and current local guidance; do not assume every patient needs routine antibiotic prophylaxis.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Page 9.

# 2. Atrial Septal Defect (ASD)

## Clinical Case 02 — A child with a fixed split second heart sound

A 6-year-old child is referred after a murmur is noticed during a routine examination. The child is active and has no obvious heart failure. Auscultation reveals a fixed wide splitting of the second heart sound and an ejection systolic murmur at the upper left sternal border. Echocardiography shows an ostium secundum ASD with right-sided chamber dilatation.

**Case focus:** ASD types, left-to-right shunt, characteristic examination, ECG/CXR patterns, closure options and complications.

### Q9. [Easy] Which diagnosis best explains the fixed wide splitting of the second heart sound?

A. Ventricular septal defect  
B. Patent ductus arteriosus  
C. Coarctation of the aorta  
D. Tetralogy of Fallot  
E. Atrial septal defect  

**Correct Answer:** E. Atrial septal defect

**Initial Explanation:**

A fixed wide split S2 is a classic examination finding in ASD. The right ventricular stroke volume changes relatively little with respiration because of the persistent left-to-right atrial shunt.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. VSD — Incorrect.** VSD classically produces a pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border.
- **B. PDA — Incorrect.** PDA is associated with a continuous machinery murmur and, when large, bounding pulses and wide pulse pressure.
- **C. Coarctation — Incorrect.** The key findings are higher blood pressure in the upper limbs and weak or delayed femoral pulses.
- **D. TOF — Incorrect.** TOF is associated with cyanosis, a pulmonary ejection systolic murmur and a single S2 when the pulmonary component is absent.
- **E. ASD — Correct.** Fixed wide splitting of S2 is characteristic of ASD.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- ASD → left-to-right shunt → right-sided volume loading.
- Fixed wide splitting of S2 is a key clinical clue.
- An ejection systolic murmur may be caused by increased flow across the pulmonary valve; it is not necessarily a murmur directly produced by blood crossing the ASD.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Pages 12–13.

### Q10. [Easy] Which type of ASD is the commonest form?

A. Ostium primum  
B. Sinus venosus  
C. Ostium secundum  
D. Coronary sinus defect  
E. Complete atrioventricular septal defect  

**Correct Answer:** C. Ostium secundum

**Initial Explanation:**

Ostium secundum is the commonest ASD type. The sheet also describes ostium primum and sinus venosus defects.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Ostium primum — Incorrect.** It is located in the lower part of the atrial septum near the atrioventricular valves and may be associated with valve abnormalities.
- **B. Sinus venosus — Incorrect.** It is located near the entry of the SVC and may be associated with partial anomalous pulmonary venous return.
- **C. Ostium secundum — Correct.** This is the commonest type of ASD.
- **D. Coronary sinus defect — Incorrect.** This is a less common atrial-level communication and is not the commonest type listed.
- **E. Complete AVSD — Incorrect.** This is a broader endocardial cushion defect involving atrial and ventricular septal components and a common AV valve, not an isolated secundum ASD.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Secundum ASD: commonest type.
- Primum ASD: lower septum; may be associated with AV-valve regurgitation.
- Sinus venosus ASD: near SVC; consider associated partial anomalous pulmonary venous return.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Page 12.

### Q11. [Moderate] Why is an ejection systolic murmur heard at the upper left sternal border in many children with ASD?

A. The VSD produces turbulence at the lower left sternal border  
B. Blood crosses the atrial defect and creates a high-velocity jet through the aortic valve  
C. The mitral valve is always severely stenosed  
D. The ductus arteriosus produces continuous flow  
E. Increased right-sided flow passes through the pulmonary valve  

**Correct Answer:** E. Increased right-sided flow passes through the pulmonary valve

**Initial Explanation:**

The left-to-right shunt increases right ventricular output. Increased flow across the pulmonary valve produces an ejection systolic flow murmur at the upper left sternal border.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** VSD produces a pansystolic murmur, typically at the lower left sternal border.
- **B. Incorrect.** The murmur is not usually a direct jet murmur across the ASD; it is a flow murmur across the pulmonary valve.
- **C. Incorrect.** Mitral stenosis is not an obligatory feature of ASD. Primum ASD may be associated with AV-valve abnormalities or mitral regurgitation.
- **D. Incorrect.** PDA produces a continuous machinery murmur in the left infraclavicular region.
- **E. Correct.** Increased pulmonary blood flow produces increased flow across the pulmonary valve and an ejection systolic murmur.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- ASD murmur: ejection systolic at the upper left sternal border due to increased pulmonary flow.
- Fixed wide split S2 is the more characteristic auscultatory clue.
- A primum defect may have an additional apical pansystolic murmur from AV-valve regurgitation.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Page 13.

### Q12. [Moderate] Which ECG pattern is most consistent with an ostium secundum ASD as described in the sheet?

A. Biventricular hypertrophy as the obligatory finding  
B. Left ventricular hypertrophy with marked left-axis deviation only  
C. Short PR interval with delta wave  
D. Complete heart block with no atrial activity  
E. Right ventricular hypertrophy, right-axis deviation and right bundle branch block  

**Correct Answer:** E. Right ventricular hypertrophy, right-axis deviation and right bundle branch block

**Initial Explanation:**

The sheet associates secundum ASD with a normal ECG or right ventricular hypertrophy, right-axis deviation and RBBB.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Biventricular hypertrophy is more consistent with a significant VSD in the sheet.
- **B. Incorrect.** The sheet describes superior or left-axis deviation and LBBB with primum ASD, not the usual secundum pattern.
- **C. Incorrect.** A short PR interval and delta wave suggest Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome.
- **D. Incorrect.** This describes a conduction disorder, not the typical ECG pattern of secundum ASD.
- **E. Correct.** This is the ECG pattern described for secundum ASD.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Secundum ASD: ECG may be normal or show RVH, RAD and RBBB.
- Primum ASD: may show RVH, superior/left-axis deviation and LBBB.
- Echocardiography confirms the defect and assesses right-sided dilatation.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Page 13.

### Q13. [Moderate] Which imaging combination may be seen in a haemodynamically significant ASD?

A. Cardiomegaly and pulmonary plethora  
B. Boot-shaped heart with absent pulmonary vascular markings  
C. Egg-on-side heart with narrow mediastinum  
D. Small heart and oligemic lung fields  
E. Rib notching with isolated pulmonary oligemia  

**Correct Answer:** A. Cardiomegaly and pulmonary plethora

**Initial Explanation:**

A significant ASD can cause right-sided volume loading and increased pulmonary blood flow. The sheet describes cardiomegaly and plethoric lungs on chest X-ray.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Correct.** These findings are compatible with a significant left-to-right shunt at atrial level.
- **B. Incorrect.** A boot-shaped heart is characteristic of TOF.
- **C. Incorrect.** An egg-on-side appearance is associated with TGA.
- **D. Incorrect.** A small heart with oligemic lung fields is classically associated with reduced pulmonary blood flow, such as in TOF.
- **E. Incorrect.** Rib notching suggests collateral circulation in coarctation and is not the expected ASD pattern.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- ASD with a significant shunt: cardiomegaly and pulmonary plethora may be present.
- ECG supports right-sided volume loading.
- Echocardiography is the diagnostic investigation.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Pages 13 and 16.

### Q14. [Hard] An asymptomatic child has a secundum ASD. Echocardiography shows significant right atrial and right ventricular dilatation. Which closure method is generally described for a suitable large secundum ASD?

A. Immediate pulmonary artery banding  
B. No intervention under any circumstances  
C. Blalock–Taussig shunt  
D. Balloon atrial septostomy  
E. Transcatheter device closure  

**Correct Answer:** E. Transcatheter device closure

**Initial Explanation:**

The sheet describes catheter-based closure with an occlusion device for a large secundum ASD. Closure is considered when the shunt causes right-sided dilatation and the anatomy is suitable.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Pulmonary artery banding is used in selected complex congenital conditions, not routine secundum ASD closure.
- **B. Incorrect.** Small defects may close spontaneously, but a significant shunt with right-sided dilatation requires specialist assessment for closure.
- **C. Incorrect.** A Blalock–Taussig shunt is a palliative systemic-to-pulmonary shunt used in selected cyanotic lesions.
- **D. Incorrect.** Balloon atrial septostomy is used to improve mixing in selected conditions such as TGA, not to close an ASD.
- **E. Correct.** Transcatheter device closure is described for secundum ASD when the anatomy is suitable.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Small ASD: spontaneous closure may occur.
- Large secundum ASD with right-sided dilatation: catheter device closure if suitable.
- Primum ASD: usually requires surgical correction because of associated AV-valve abnormalities.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Page 13.

### Q15. [Hard] A child has an ostium primum ASD with associated mitral regurgitation. Which treatment approach best matches the sheet?

A. Indomethacin to close the defect  
B. Surgical correction  
C. Transcatheter occlusion device as the routine method for all primum defects  
D. Balloon pulmonary valvotomy  
E. No follow-up because primum defects are always benign

Ostium primum ASD is associated with the atrioventricular septal region and may involve AV-valve abnormalities. The sheet describes surgical correction for large primum defects.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Indomethacin is used in selected preterm infants to promote ductal closure in PDA, not to close ASD.
- **B. Correct.** Surgical correction addresses the primum defect and associated AV-valve abnormality.
- **C. Incorrect.** Device closure is described for suitable secundum ASD, not routinely for primum defects with AV-valve involvement.
- **D. Incorrect.** Pulmonary valvotomy treats pulmonary valve stenosis.
- **E. Incorrect.** A primum defect with valve regurgitation requires cardiology follow-up and assessment for repair.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Primum ASD: lower atrial septum and possible AV-valve/mitral regurgitation.
- Secundum ASD: device closure may be suitable.
- Primum ASD: surgical repair is described in the sheet.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Pages 12–13.

# 3. Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA)

## Clinical Case 03 — The infant with bounding pulses

A 6-week-old infant is reviewed for poor weight gain and recurrent respiratory symptoms. The infant has tachypnoea and feeding difficulty. Examination reveals bounding pulses, a wide pulse pressure and a continuous machinery murmur below the left clavicle. Echocardiography confirms a large patent ductus arteriosus.

**Case focus:** PDA physiology, clinical signs, investigations, management in preterm versus full-term infants, and complications.

### Q17. [Easy] Which diagnosis is most likely?

A. Pulmonary stenosis  
B. Atrial septal defect  
C. Ventricular septal defect  
D. Patent ductus arteriosus  
E. Coarctation of the aorta  

**Correct Answer:** D. Patent ductus arteriosus

**Initial Explanation:**

A continuous machinery murmur in the left infraclavicular area, bounding pulses and wide pulse pressure are characteristic of a significant PDA.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Pulmonary stenosis — Incorrect.** It produces an ejection systolic murmur at the pulmonary area and may cause right ventricular hypertrophy.
- **B. ASD — Incorrect.** ASD is associated with fixed wide splitting of S2 and an ejection systolic flow murmur at the upper left sternal border.
- **C. VSD — Incorrect.** VSD typically produces a pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border.
- **D. PDA — Correct.** Persistent communication between the aorta and pulmonary artery causes continuous left-to-right flow after birth.
- **E. Coarctation — Incorrect.** It produces upper–lower limb blood-pressure differences and weak femoral pulses.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- PDA → continuous machinery murmur, usually left infraclavicular.
- Large PDA → bounding/collapsing pulses and wide pulse pressure.
- Large shunt may cause pulmonary congestion, heart failure and failure to thrive.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Pages 14–16.

### Q18. [Easy] What is the direction of the shunt in a typical postnatal PDA?

A. Left to right  
B. Right to left  
C. Bidirectional in every patient  
D. Pulmonary artery to right atrium  
E. No shunt occurs after birth  

**Correct Answer:** A. Left to right

**Initial Explanation:**

Aortic pressure is higher than pulmonary arterial pressure after birth, so blood passes from the aorta into the pulmonary artery through the persistent ductus, increasing pulmonary blood flow.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Correct.** The usual postnatal PDA produces a left-to-right shunt.
- **B. Incorrect.** Right-to-left ductal flow can occur in selected conditions with high pulmonary vascular pressure, but it is not the usual uncomplicated PDA pattern.
- **C. Incorrect.** Bidirectional flow is not the defining pattern in an uncomplicated PDA.
- **D. Incorrect.** The ductus connects the aorta and pulmonary artery, not the pulmonary artery and right atrium.
- **E. Incorrect.** A patent ductus represents persistent communication and permits blood flow.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Typical PDA: aorta → pulmonary artery.
- The shunt increases pulmonary blood flow and can overload the left heart.
- Advanced pulmonary vascular disease can eventually cause shunt reversal.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Pages 14–16.

### Q19. [Moderate] Which finding is the best explanation for the wide pulse pressure in a large PDA?

A. Complete obstruction of the pulmonary valve  
B. Increased venous return from the lower limbs only  
C. Reduced stroke volume in all cardiac chambers  
D. Diastolic runoff of blood from the aorta into the pulmonary artery  
E. Increased diastolic pressure due to reduced runoff  

**Correct Answer:** D. Diastolic runoff of blood from the aorta into the pulmonary artery

**Initial Explanation:**

Blood continues to leave the aorta through the ductus during diastole. This lowers diastolic pressure, while the increased stroke volume may raise systolic pressure, producing a wide pulse pressure.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Pulmonary valve obstruction does not explain the characteristic wide pulse pressure of PDA.
- **B. Incorrect.** Venous return from the lower limbs is not the mechanism of the wide pulse pressure.
- **C. Incorrect.** A large PDA causes a hyperdynamic circulation rather than uniform reduction in stroke volume.
- **D. Correct.** Aortic-to-pulmonary runoff during diastole is the key mechanism.
- **E. Incorrect.** The sheet describes a fall in diastolic pressure due to diastolic runoff, not an increase.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Large PDA → diastolic runoff → low diastolic BP.
- Increased systolic flow and low diastolic pressure → wide pulse pressure.
- Bounding/collapsing pulse is a clinical correlate.

**Source:** CVS sheet — Page 15.

### Q30. [Easy] Which diagnosis best explains the upper–lower limb pulse and blood-pressure discrepancy?

D. Tetralogy of Fallot  
E. Atrial septal defect  

**Correct Answer:** B. Coarctation of the aorta

**Initial Explanation:**

Coarctation causes narrowing of the aorta, usually near the ductal region. Blood pressure is elevated proximal to the narrowing and reduced distal to it, producing weak femoral pulses and an upper–lower limb pressure difference.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Pulmonary stenosis — Incorrect.** Pulmonary stenosis causes right ventricular outflow obstruction and a pulmonary area ejection systolic murmur.

- **D. TOF — Incorrect.** TOF produces cyanosis and reduced pulmonary blood flow rather than the classic four-limb pressure discrepancy.
- **E. ASD — Incorrect.** ASD is characterized by fixed wide splitting of S2 and right-sided volume loading.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- CoA: upper-limb BP higher than lower-limb BP.
- Femoral pulses are weak or delayed.
- Measure BP in all four limbs when CoA is suspected.

A. Dilated cardiomyopathy with thin walls; digitalis is the preferred inotrope  
B. Restrictive cardiomyopathy; beta-blockers are contraindicated  
C. Large VSD with pulmonary plethora; indomethacin is indicated  
D. Tetralogy of Fallot; prostaglandin E should be started  
E. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; digitalis is contraindicated if there is outflow obstruction  

**Correct Answer:** E. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; digitalis is contraindicated if there is outflow obstruction

**Initial Explanation:**

Maternal diabetes (infant of a diabetic mother) is a recognised cause of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, characterised by a thick wall with reduced compliance. Positive inotropes such as digitalis increase contractility and are contraindicated when there is obstruction.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Dilated cardiomyopathy has a dilated, thin-walled ventricle with reduced contractility — the opposite of the echo described.
- **B. Incorrect.** Restrictive cardiomyopathy is very rare, with stiff small/normal ventricles; the question describes marked wall thickening, and beta-blockers are among the drugs used in hypertrophic disease.
- **C. Incorrect.** A VSD would not explain a thickened septum with systolic outflow narrowing, and indomethacin is used for PDA in preterm infants.
- **D. Incorrect.** Maternal diabetes is also linked with VSD and TOF, but TOF is a cyanotic lesion with pulmonary stenosis, not diffuse hypertrophy with reduced compliance; PGE is used for duct-dependent lesions.
- **E. Correct.** HCM = thick wall + reduced compliance (diastolic dysfunction). Management: avoid severe exertion, beta-blockers; digitalis is contraindicated in obstructive lesions such as HOCM.
- **Clinical reasoning:** The key clue is the maternal diabetes — it links the infant to the cardiomyopathy and the drug caution.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Maternal diabetes → HCM, VSD, TOF.
- Obstructive CHD (e.g., aortic stenosis) and metabolic disease are also causes of HCM.
- HCM complications: chest pain, arrhythmia, syncope, sudden death.
- Digitalis is contraindicated in obstructive lesions (e.g., HOCM).

## Clinical Case 26 — Large shunts and their late consequences

### Q149. [Medium] A 3-month-old with a large VSD has tachypnoea, sweating on feeds and poor weight gain. In a large VSD the increased pulmonary blood flow returns to the left atrium and crosses the mitral valve in larger volume, producing a relative (functional) mitral diastolic murmur. The increased flow across the pulmonary valve produces the ejection systolic murmur.

- **A. Correct.** Apical mid-diastolic murmur from increased flow across the mitral valve; the ESM reflects increased flow across the pulmonary valve (functional pulmonary stenosis).

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Large VSD: soft pansystolic murmur ± functional ESM at the pulmonary area ± apical mid-diastolic murmur.

On examination the pansystolic murmur is softer than before, the second heart sound is loud and palpable, and there is a right ventricular heave. Which interpretation is most appropriate?

A. The defect is closing spontaneously and follow-up can be stopped  
B. Heart failure has resolved because the shunt has reduced

## Clinical Case 27 — Large shunts and their late consequences

### Q151. [Medium] A preterm infant of 28 weeks has a haemodynamically significant PDA and is treated in the first week with indomethacin and restriction of intravenous fluids. On day 7 she develops abdominal distension, bilious aspirates and blood in the stools. Which adverse effect of the therapy is most consistent?

C. Digoxin toxicity  
D. Diuretic-induced hypokalaemia  
E. Rupture of the ductus arteriosus  

**Correct Answer:** B. Necrotising enterocolitis

**Initial Explanation:**

Anti-prostaglandin therapy (indomethacin or ibuprofen) closes the ductus but can cause necrotising enterocolitis, bleeding and renal failure. The picture of abdominal distension, bilious aspirates and bloody stools suggests NEC.

# 18. Syncope and Pulmonary Hypertension

## Clinical Case 28 — Large shunts and their late consequences

### Q152. [Hard] A 23-year-old woman with an unrepaired large VSD from childhood has progressive dyspnoea, cyanosis, haemoptysis and finger clubbing. She has a palpable, loud second heart sound and a right ventricular heave. Which set of investigation results is most consistent with her diagnosis?

D. Polycythaemia; egg-on-side heart; normal ECG; parallel circulations  
E. Polycythaemia; cardiomegaly with oligaemic lung fields; RVH with a tall peaked P wave; right-to-left shunt with high pulmonary pressure on Doppler

# 16. Infective Endocarditis (IE)

## Clinical Case 45 — Fever in a child with structural heart disease

### Q182. [Medium] A 10-year-old with an unrepaired VSD has had fever and fatigue for 3 weeks. On examination there are tender nodules on the finger pulps, painless flat red macules on the palms, splinter haemorrhages and splenomegaly. Urine microscopy shows haematuria and the character of the murmur has changed. Which statement is correct?

A. Painful nodules are Janeway lesions and painless palm lesions are Osler nodes  
B. The haematuria indicates an unrelated urinary infection  
C. Painful nodules are Osler nodes and painless macules are Janeway lesions; haematuria suggests immune-complex glomerulonephritis, which supports infective endocarditis  
D. Splenomegaly excludes infective endocarditis  
E. A normal echocardiogram would exclude infective endocarditis  

**Correct Answer:** C

**Initial Explanation:**

Osler nodes are painful and Janeway lesions are painless. Splinter haemorrhages, splenomegaly and glomerulonephritis (microscopic haematuria) are recognised features, and a new or changed murmur is the key cardiac sign.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** The two are reversed.
- **B. Incorrect.** In the setting of IE, haematuria reflects glomerulonephritis; low C3/C4 may be found.
- **C. Correct.**
- **D. Incorrect.** Splenomegaly is found in about 70%.
- **E. Incorrect.** Absence of vegetations does not exclude IE.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Signs: high fever, chills, anorexia, weight loss, pallor, clubbing (late), splinter haemorrhages, Osler nodes, Janeway lesions, Roth spots, subconjunctival haemorrhage, splenomegaly, GN, hemiplegia.
- Cardiac: new murmur, change in an existing murmur, heart failure.
- Blood cultures (at least 3 before antibiotics), echo, CRP/ESR, urine, C3/C4.

**Source:** Infective endocarditis — clinical picture and diagnosis (sheet p. 43).

### Q183. [Hard] A 12-year-old with an unrepaired VSD has a temperature of 39°C. One of three blood-culture bottles grows viridans streptococci. Echocardiography shows a vegetation, and Osler nodes are present. How is this case classified using the Duke criteria in the sheet?

A. Definite IE: one major criterion (vegetation on echo) plus at least three minor criteria (fever, predisposing heart lesion, single positive culture, immune-complex phenomenon)  
B. Possible IE only  
C. IE is rejected because only one blood culture is positive  
D. Definite IE only if two major criteria are present  
E. Cannot be classified without a CRP result  

**Correct Answer:** A

**Initial Explanation:**

Definite IE requires two major criteria, or one major plus three minor, or five minor criteria. A single positive culture is a minor criterion; positive echo is a major criterion.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Correct.** Major: echo vegetation. Minor: fever, predisposing factor (VSD), single positive culture, immune-complex phenomenon (Osler nodes).
- **B. Incorrect.** The case meets the definite threshold.
- **C. Incorrect.** One positive culture counts as a minor criterion; it does not reject the diagnosis.
- **D. Incorrect.** One major + three minor is also definite.
- **E. Incorrect.** CRP is not part of the Duke criteria.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Major: ≥ 2 positive blood cultures; positive echo (vegetation, new valve damage).
- Minor: fever, embolic phenomenon, immune-complex disease, predisposing factors, single positive culture.
- Definite: 2 major, or 1 major + 3 minor, or 5 minor.

**Source:** Infective endocarditis — Duke criteria (sheet p. 44).

# 17. Cardiac Arrhythmias — SVT and Conduction Disorders

## Clinical Case 46 — Syncope, palpitations and irregular pulses

### Q187. [Medium] A 9-year-old boy with congenital deafness has recurrent exercise-related syncope. The ECG shows a prolonged QT interval. His parents are consanguineous and an older brother died suddenly at the age of 7. Which diagnosis is most likely?

A. Romano–Ward syndrome  
B. Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome  
C. Lange–Nielsen (Jervell and Lange-Nielsen) syndrome  
D. Congenital complete heart block  
E. Supraventricular tachycardia  

**Correct Answer:** C

**Initial Explanation:**

Lange–Nielsen syndrome is the autosomal recessive form of congenital long-QT syndrome associated with deafness. Romano–Ward syndrome is autosomal dominant without deafness.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Autosomal dominant, normal hearing.
- **B. Incorrect.** WPW shows a short PR interval and delta wave.
- **C. Correct.**
- **D. Incorrect.** CHB relates to maternal anti-Ro/La antibodies and bradycardia.
- **E. Incorrect.** SVT: narrow QRS tachycardia, normal ECG between attacks.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- LQTS: syncope (often exercise), seizures (misdiagnosed as epilepsy), sudden death.
- ECG: QT > 0.47 s. Treatment: propranolol, ICD.

**Source:** Long QT syndrome (sheet p. 46).

### Q188. [Hard] A 7-year-old is treated with erythromycin for pneumonia and also has vomiting and diarrhoea. She has two episodes of loss of consciousness with jerking movements that were labelled as seizures. The ECG shows a QT interval of 0.50 s and the serum potassium is low. There is no family history. ### Q189. [Medium] A 3-month-old had 12 hours of pallor, irritability and vomiting and was found to have a heart rate of 280/min with a narrow QRS and no visible P waves. He converted after vagal stimulation and adenosine, and the ECG in sinus rhythm is normal. The parents ask about the future. Which advice is most accurate?

A. Lifelong antiarrhythmic drugs and ablation will be required  
B. No follow-up is needed because SVT never recurs  
C. Echocardiography is unnecessary

E. A permanent pacemaker will be required

- **D. Correct.**

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**
