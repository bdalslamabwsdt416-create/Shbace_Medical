### Q1. [Easy] Which urine description is most suggestive of glomerular haematuria?

A. Orange urine after rifampicin  
B. Tea- or cola-coloured urine with dysmorphic red cells  
C. Cloudy urine with offensive smell only  
D. Bright-red urine with isomorphic red cells and clots  
E. Black urine due to homogentisic acid  

**Correct Answer:** B. Tea- or cola-coloured urine with dysmorphic red cells

**Initial Explanation:**

Glomerular bleeding typically produces tea- or cola-coloured urine because blood mixes with urine during passage through the nephron. Red cells become dysmorphic after crossing the glomerular filtration barrier.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Orange urine after rifampicin — Incorrect.** Rifampicin can discolor urine but does not indicate haematuria.
- **B. Tea/cola urine with dysmorphic cells — Correct.** This is the characteristic glomerular pattern described in the sheet.
- **C. Cloudy offensive urine — Incorrect.** This suggests UTI but does not establish glomerular bleeding.
- **D. Bright-red urine with isomorphic cells and clots — Incorrect.** This pattern suggests an extraglomerular source, such as lower urinary tract bleeding.
- **E. Black urine — Incorrect.** The sheet lists homogentisic acid in alkaptonuria as one possible cause of dark urine.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Glomerular haematuria: tea/cola colour, dysmorphic RBCs, RBC casts and often proteinuria.
- Extraglomerular haematuria: bright red, isomorphic RBCs and possible clots.
- Always distinguish true haematuria from pigmenturia and drug/food-related urine discoloration.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Pages 5–6.

### Q2. [Moderate] Which urine microscopy finding most strongly supports a glomerular source of bleeding?

A. Isomorphic red cells without casts  
B. Squamous epithelial cells only  
C. Crystals alone  
D. Bacteria with nitrite positivity  
E. Red-cell casts  

**Correct Answer:** E. Red-cell casts

**Initial Explanation:**

Red-cell casts form within renal tubules and strongly support bleeding originating within the nephron, particularly glomerular inflammation.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Isomorphic RBCs — Incorrect.** These are more consistent with an extraglomerular source.
- **B. Squamous epithelial cells — Incorrect.** They may indicate contamination of the sample.
- **C. Crystals — Incorrect.** They may occur with stones or metabolic abnormalities but do not establish glomerular bleeding.
- **D. Bacteria and nitrites — Incorrect.** These support bacterial UTI rather than glomerular haematuria.
- **E. Red-cell casts — Correct.** They indicate that red cells have entered the tubular lumen and formed casts, supporting glomerular disease.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- RBC casts usually indicate glomerular disease.
- WBC casts suggest pyelonephritis.
- Hyaline casts may be seen in normal urine, fever, nephrotic syndrome or heart failure.
- Granular casts may be seen in renal parenchymal disease.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 6.

### Q3. [Moderate] A urine sample is needed urgently from a severely ill young infant. Which collection method provides a relatively uncontaminated specimen directly from the bladder?

E. Clean-catch sample after nappy removal

Suprapubic aspiration obtains urine directly from the bladder using a needle. The sheet describes it as an option in infants or newborns when urgent diagnosis is required, although it is invasive.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Suprapubic aspiration — Correct.** It provides a direct bladder sample and may be used in a severely ill infant requiring urgent diagnosis.
- **B. Unclean midstream sample — Incorrect.** Proper cleansing and collection technique are important to reduce contamination.
- **C. Adhesive bag — Incorrect.** It is non-invasive but has a higher contamination risk from skin or faeces.
- **D. Nappy sample — Incorrect.** It is unsuitable for reliable urine culture because of contamination.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Clean catch: recommended non-invasive method when feasible.
- Midstream urine: preferable in school-age children.
- SPA: direct and relatively uncontaminated, but invasive.
- Bag specimens have contamination limitations and must be interpreted cautiously.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 4.

D. Glomerular capillaries  
E. Minimal change nephropathy

Bright-red urine, isomorphic red cells and clots point toward bleeding outside the glomerulus, such as the ureter, bladder or urethra.

- Extraglomerular: bright red, isomorphic RBCs, clots.
- The history should establish whether haematuria is initial, terminal or throughout the stream.

# 2. Vesicoureteric Reflux (VUR)

## Clinical Case 02 — Recurrent febrile UTIs and renal scarring

A 3-year-old girl has had three episodes of febrile UTI. Renal ultrasound shows urinary tract dilatation. Micturating cystourethrography demonstrates retrograde flow of urine from the bladder into both ureters and the renal pelvis. The clinician explains that recurrent infection and raised voiding pressure may damage the renal parenchyma.

**Case focus:** VUR definition, primary and secondary causes, complications, imaging, grading, management and follow-up.

### Q8. [Easy] What is the defining feature of vesicoureteric reflux?

A. Protein leakage through the glomerular filtration barrier  
B. Failure of glomerular filtration  
C. Forward flow of urine from the kidney into the bladder  
D. Obstruction at the renal artery  
E. Retrograde flow of urine from the bladder into the ureter and renal pelvis  

**Correct Answer:** E. Retrograde flow of urine from the bladder into the ureter and renal pelvis

**Initial Explanation:**

VUR is the abnormal retrograde passage of urine from the bladder into the upper urinary tract.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** This describes proteinuria due to filtration-barrier dysfunction.
- **B. Incorrect.** This describes impaired kidney filtration, not reflux.
- **C. Incorrect.** This is the normal direction of urine flow.
- **D. Incorrect.** Renal artery obstruction causes vascular impairment and may contribute to hypertension.
- **E. Correct.** This is the definition of VUR in the sheet.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- VUR: bladder → ureter/renal pelvis.
- Primary VUR is related to an abnormal vesicoureteric junction.
- Secondary VUR may occur with recurrent UTI, urinary obstruction or neuropathic bladder.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 7.

### Q9. [Moderate] Which of the following is a cause of secondary VUR?

A. Alport syndrome  
B. Congenital abnormality of the vesicoureteric junction  
C. Isolated minimal change nephropathy  
D. Recurrent cystitis or urinary obstruction  
E. Renal agenesis  

**Correct Answer:** D. Recurrent cystitis or urinary obstruction

**Initial Explanation:**

The sheet distinguishes primary VUR, caused by an abnormal vesicoureteric junction, from secondary reflux due to conditions such as recurrent UTI, posterior urethral valves or neuropathic bladder.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Alport syndrome — Incorrect.** It is a type IV collagen disorder causing hereditary nephritis.
- **B. Congenital junction abnormality — Incorrect.** This is the mechanism of primary VUR.
- **C. Minimal change nephropathy — Incorrect.** It is a glomerular protein-losing disease.
- **D. Recurrent cystitis or obstruction — Correct.** These are listed causes of secondary VUR.
- **E. Renal agenesis — Incorrect.** It is a congenital renal developmental anomaly, not the usual mechanism of secondary reflux.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Primary VUR: abnormal vesicoureteric junction; often familial.
- Secondary VUR: recurrent UTI, obstruction (e.g. PUV), neuropathic bladder or previous procedures.
- Ask about recurrent febrile UTI and voiding dysfunction.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 7.

### Q10. [Moderate] How can longstanding severe VUR damage the kidney?

A. Reflux transmits voiding pressure to the renal pelvis and promotes infection, scarring and hydronephrosis  
B. It prevents urine stasis  
C. It increases glomerular basement membrane thickness  
D. It reduces bladder pressure during voiding  
E. It causes immediate complete renal agenesis  

**Correct Answer:** A. Reflux transmits voiding pressure to the renal pelvis and promotes infection, scarring and hydronephrosis

**Initial Explanation:**

VUR can cause urinary stasis and recurrent infection. Pressure transmitted during voiding may dilate the upper urinary tract and damage renal tissue, leading to reflux nephropathy.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Correct.** This explains the pathway to pyelonephritis, renal scarring, hypertension and CKD.
- **B. Incorrect.** VUR may contribute to incomplete emptying and urinary stasis.
- **C. Incorrect.** This is not the mechanism of reflux nephropathy.
- **D. Incorrect.** Reflux does not protect the renal pelvis from pressure.
- **E. Incorrect.** Renal agenesis is a congenital failure of development and cannot be caused by VUR.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- VUR → stasis/recurrent infection + transmitted pressure → renal scarring.
- Severe bilateral reflux nephropathy may lead to hypertension and CKD.
- Recurrent pyelonephritis is a major pathway to renal damage.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 7.

### Q11. [Easy] Which investigation is used to diagnose and grade VUR?

A. Renal biopsy  
B. Serum creatinine alone  
C. MCUG (micturating cystourethrogram)  
D. KUB X-ray  
E. DMSA scan  

**Correct Answer:** C. MCUG

**Initial Explanation:**

MCUG involves introducing contrast into the bladder and imaging during filling and voiding. It demonstrates reflux and helps grade its severity.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Renal biopsy — Incorrect.** It is invasive and used for selected renal parenchymal diagnoses.
- **B. Serum creatinine — Incorrect.** It assesses renal function but cannot demonstrate reflux.
- **C. MCUG — Correct.** It is the investigation used for VUR diagnosis and grading in the sheet.
- **D. KUB — Incorrect.** It may identify some stones or spinal abnormalities but does not diagnose reflux.
- **E. DMSA — Incorrect.** It is a static renal scan used to identify cortical defects and scars.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Ultrasound: anatomy and urinary tract dilatation.
- MCUG: VUR and urethral obstruction.
- DMSA: renal cortical scars.
- MAG3/DTPA renogram: dynamic drainage and obstruction assessment.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Pages 7–8.

### Q12. [Moderate] Which statement best describes Grade I VUR?

A. Reflux with gross dilatation and tortuosity of the ureter  
B. Reflux only into the bladder neck  
C. Reflux into a severely dilated collecting system with loss of papillary impressions  
D. Reflux into a non-dilated ureter  
E. No reflux is present  

**Correct Answer:** D. Reflux into a non-dilated ureter

**Initial Explanation:**

Grade I is the mildest VUR grade in the sheet and involves reflux into a non-dilated ureter.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Marked dilatation and tortuosity correspond to severe reflux.
- **B. Incorrect.** This does not define Grade I reflux.
- **C. Incorrect.** This describes the severe end of the VUR grading scale.
- **D. Correct.** This is the Grade I description.
- **E. Incorrect.** No reflux would mean no VUR.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Grade I: reflux into a non-dilated ureter.
- Grade II: reflux reaches the upper collecting system without dilatation.
- Grades IV–V: increasing dilatation; Grade V includes marked ureteral dilatation/tortuosity and loss of papillary impressions.
- Grade is determined by MCUG.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 8.

### Q13. [Moderate] What is the general approach described for low-grade VUR (Grades I–III) in the sheet?

The sheet states that Grades I–III may disappear spontaneously and may be managed with follow-up and prophylactic antibiotics in selected patients.

- **A. Incorrect.** Steroids do not correct the abnormal vesicoureteric junction.
- **B. Incorrect.** Dialysis is not indicated for reflux itself.
- **C. Incorrect.** Nephrectomy is not a treatment for uncomplicated low-grade VUR.
- **D. Incorrect.** Surgery is reserved for selected severe or persistent cases, not every low-grade reflux.
- **E. Correct.** This is the conservative approach described.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Grades I–III may resolve spontaneously.
- The sheet mentions low-dose trimethoprim as a commonly used prophylactic option, with alternatives.
- Management must be individualized to recurrence, renal scarring and the child's clinical course.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 8.

### Q14. [Hard] A child has Grade V VUR with marked ureteric dilatation and recurrent febrile UTIs despite appropriate conservative management. Which intervention is described for severe VUR in the sheet?

- **D. Incorrect.** Severe reflux with recurrent febrile UTI and renal-risk features requires active specialist follow-up.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

# 3. Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)

## Clinical Case 03 — Fever in an infant with no clear source

A 7-month-old infant presents with fever, vomiting, poor feeding and irritability. There is no obvious respiratory focus. Urine analysis shows pyuria and bacteriuria, and urine culture grows Escherichia coli. The clinician must distinguish upper from lower UTI, determine whether the infection is atypical, and choose the appropriate investigations and follow-up.

**Case focus:** Age-dependent presentation, risk factors, organisms, urine tests, pyelonephritis, imaging, treatment principles and prevention.

### Q15. [Easy] What is the most common causative organism of childhood UTI in the sheet?

A. Pseudomonas  
B. Staphylococcus aureus  
C. Klebsiella  
D. Proteus  
E. Escherichia coli  

**Correct Answer:** E. Escherichia coli

**Initial Explanation:**

E. coli, usually originating from intestinal flora and ascending through the urethra, is listed as the most common cause of childhood UTI.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Pseudomonas — Incorrect.** It is particularly associated with urinary tract abnormalities, long-standing catheterization or neurogenic bladder.
- **B. Staphylococcus aureus — Incorrect.** It is among other possible organisms, not the most common.
- **C. Klebsiella — Incorrect.** It is noted particularly in newborns but is not the commonest overall.
- **D. Proteus — Incorrect.** It is an important cause and is noted as more common in boys, but it is not the commonest organism in the sheet.
- **E. E. coli — Correct.** The sheet lists it as the most common organism, approximately 80%.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- E. coli is the commonest organism in childhood UTI.
- Proteus is associated with alkaline urine because it splits urea to ammonia.
- Ascending infection is the commonest route; blood-borne spread is important in the neonatal period.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 13.

### Q16. [Easy] Which presentation is more typical of UTI in neonates and young infants than in older children?

A. Loin pain with local tenderness  
B. Isolated suprapubic pain  
C. Non-specific symptoms such as poor feeding, irritability and vomiting  
D. Urgency with daytime frequency only  
E. Isolated dysuria with frequency  

**Correct Answer:** C. Non-specific symptoms such as poor feeding, irritability and vomiting

**Initial Explanation:**

Neonates and infants often present with non-specific features, including fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, lethargy, irritability, poor feeding or faltering growth.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Loin pain — Incorrect.** This is a more localizing symptom of pyelonephritis in older children.
- **B. Suprapubic pain — Incorrect.** It is a more specific symptom of lower UTI in older children.
- **C. Non-specific infant symptoms — Correct.** These are listed for neonates and infants in the sheet.
- **D. Urgency and frequency — Incorrect.** These are more typical of older children who can describe urinary symptoms.
- **E. Dysuria and frequency — Incorrect.** These are more specific urinary symptoms in older children.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Infants: often non-specific symptoms and may have poor feeding or faltering growth.
- Older children: dysuria, urgency, frequency, loin pain or suprapubic pain.
- UTI should remain in the differential of unexplained fever in an infant.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 13.

### Q17. [Moderate] Which feature most strongly suggests upper UTI (pyelonephritis) rather than isolated cystitis in an older child?

A. Isolated urgency  
B. Mild suprapubic discomfort only  
C. High fever with loin pain and tenderness  
D. Secondary enuresis without fever or pain  
E. Dysuria and frequency without systemic symptoms  

**Correct Answer:** C. High fever with loin pain and tenderness

**Initial Explanation:**

High fever, rigors and loin pain/tenderness suggest renal parenchymal involvement (pyelonephritis), whereas cystitis more commonly causes dysuria, frequency, urgency and suprapubic pain.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Urgency alone is more typical of lower urinary tract symptoms.
- **B. Incorrect.** Suprapubic discomfort is typical of cystitis.
- **C. Correct.** Fever with loin pain and tenderness supports upper UTI.
- **D. Incorrect.** Secondary enuresis can occur with lower UTI but does not by itself indicate pyelonephritis.
- **E. Incorrect.** These symptoms are typical of lower UTI/cystitis.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Pyelonephritis: fever, rigors, loin pain/tenderness, vomiting and systemic illness.
- Cystitis: dysuria, frequency, urgency, suprapubic pain and sometimes haematuria.
- Infants may have nonspecific presentations of upper UTI.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 13.

### Q18. [Moderate] Which result is diagnostic for UTI in the supplied sheet?

A. Ultrasound alone  
B. Serum creatinine alone  
C. DMSA scan alone  
D. Urine culture and sensitivity  
E. Urine dipstick nitrite alone in every circumstance  

**Correct Answer:** D. Urine culture and sensitivity

**Initial Explanation:**

Urine culture and sensitivity is the diagnostic test for UTI. Dipstick and microscopy support the diagnosis, but culture identifies the organism and antibiotic susceptibility.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Ultrasound — Incorrect.** It assesses anatomy and dilatation, not the microbiological diagnosis.
- **B. Serum creatinine — Incorrect.** It assesses renal function rather than proving UTI.
- **C. DMSA — Incorrect.** It is used to assess renal cortical defects/scarring.
- **D. Urine culture and sensitivity — Correct.** This is the diagnostic test identified in the sheet.
- **E. Nitrite alone — Incorrect.** A positive nitrite supports UTI, but a negative result does not exclude it and culture is required.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Urinalysis: WBCs, nitrites, leukocyte esterase and bacteria.
- Nitrite positive supports UTI; a negative nitrite does not exclude infection.
- Culture confirms the organism and guides antibiotic sensitivity.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Pages 6 and 14.

### Q19. [Moderate] Which finding makes a UTI atypical and supports more extensive investigation according to the sheet?

E. Normal renal function and normal ultrasound  

**Correct Answer:** B. Poor urine flow or failure to respond to suitable antibiotics within 48 hours

**Initial Explanation:**

The sheet lists serious illness, septicaemia, poor urine flow, abdominal or bladder mass, raised creatinine, non-E. coli organisms, poor response within 48 hours and a family history of renal anomalies as reasons for extensive investigation.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. E. coli in a well child — Incorrect.** E. coli is the commonest usual organism.
- **B. Poor flow or poor response — Correct.** Both are listed as atypical features.
- **C. First uncomplicated cystitis — Incorrect.** It does not by itself represent an atypical infection in the sheet.
- **D. Rapid response — Incorrect.** This is reassuring rather than an atypical feature.
- **E. Normal tests — Incorrect.** Normal findings do not create an indication for extensive investigation.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Atypical UTI: serious illness/septicaemia, poor flow, mass, raised creatinine, non-E. coli organism or failure to respond within 48 hours.
- Recurrent UTI also prompts further assessment.
- Consider structural abnormalities such as VUR or obstruction.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 14.

### Q22. [Moderate] Which measure is included in the sheet for prevention of recurrent childhood UTI?

D. Routine nephrectomy

### Q222. [Moderate] Which acid–base pattern is most consistent with renal tubular acidosis in the supplied sheet?

B. Respiratory acidosis  
C. Respiratory alkalosis  
D. Normal-anion-gap, hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis  
E. High anion gap metabolic alkalosis with hyperkalaemia  

**Correct Answer:** D. Normal-anion-gap, hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis

**Initial Explanation:**

RTA is described as a chronic normal-anion-gap metabolic acidosis, typically with hyperchloraemia. Potassium is often low in types I and II.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** RTA produces acidosis, not alkalosis.
- **B. Incorrect.** Respiratory acidosis is caused by inadequate carbon dioxide elimination.
- **C. Incorrect.** Respiratory alkalosis is not the primary acid–base disorder described.

- **E. Incorrect.** Type IV RTA can involve hyperkalaemia, but the defining acid–base disturbance remains metabolic acidosis, not alkalosis.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Types I and II are commonly associated with hypokalaemia; type IV is associated with hyperkalaemia.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Pages 39 and 41.

### Q223. [Hard] The child's urine pH remains above 5.5 despite systemic acidosis, and nephrocalcinosis is present. Which tubular defect is most likely?

D. Increased aldosterone effect  
E. Isolated urinary infection  

**Correct Answer:** A. Failure of distal hydrogen-ion secretion (type I distal RTA)

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Correct.** Failure to excrete hydrogen ions explains the inappropriately high urine pH during systemic acidosis.
- **B. Incorrect.** Reduced filtration alone does not define distal RTA.
- **C. Incorrect.** Proximal bicarbonate handling is the main defect in type II RTA.
- **D. Incorrect.** Increased aldosterone effect does not explain distal acidification failure.
- **E. Incorrect.** UTI may alter urine pH but does not explain the persistent tubular acidification defect and clinical pattern.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Type I distal RTA: failure of H⁺ excretion by the distal tubule.
- Urine pH may remain above 5.5–6.0.
- The sheet links distal RTA with renal stones/nephrocalcinosis.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Pages 39–40.

### Q224. [Moderate] Another child has proximal bicarbonate wasting. As systemic acidosis becomes established, which urine pH behaviour is possible in type II RTA?

- **A. Incorrect.** Persistently high urine pH despite systemic acidosis is more characteristic of distal RTA.
- **B. Correct.** The sheet notes that urine pH can fall below 5.5 in proximal RTA.
- **C. Incorrect.** Urine pH varies with the acid–base state and tubular response.
- **D. Incorrect.** This is not a defining feature of proximal RTA.
- **E. Incorrect.** Urine pH does not diagnose nephrotic syndrome.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Type II proximal RTA: failure of bicarbonate reabsorption by the proximal tubule.
- Urine pH can fall below 5.5 after serum bicarbonate has fallen.

**Source:** Nephrology sheet — Page 39.
