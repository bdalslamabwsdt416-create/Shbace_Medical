### Q1. [Easy] Which statement best defines a seizure?

A. A transient occurrence of symptoms or signs due to abnormal electrical activity in the brain  
B. Any involuntary limb movement during sleep  
C. Any episode of fever associated with irritability  
D. A permanent loss of motor function caused by a structural brain lesion  
E. A prolonged state of unconsciousness caused only by hypoglycaemia  

**Correct Answer:** A. A transient occurrence of symptoms or signs due to abnormal electrical activity in the brain

**Initial Explanation:**

A seizure is a transient clinical event caused by abnormal electrical activity in the brain. It may manifest with motor, sensory, autonomic, behavioural or consciousness changes, or a combination of these.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Correct.** This is the definition given in the lecture.
- **B. Incorrect.** Involuntary movement during sleep is not necessarily epileptic; normal sleep movements and other paroxysmal disorders may mimic seizures.
- **C. Incorrect.** Fever and irritability alone do not define a seizure.
- **D. Incorrect.** A seizure is transient and does not by itself mean permanent motor loss.
- **E. Incorrect.** Hypoglycaemia can provoke a seizure, but seizures have many causes and are not defined by prolonged unconsciousness.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Seizure: transient symptoms/signs due to abnormal brain electrical activity.
- Manifestations may be motor, sensory, autonomic, behavioural/psychic or mixed.
- Convulsion refers specifically to involuntary muscular contraction (a motor manifestation); not every seizure is convulsive.
- Febrile convulsion is the commonest cause of childhood convulsions in the lecture.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Page 3.

### Q2. [Moderate] Which child meets the lecture's definition of a febrile seizure?

A. A 4-month-old infant with fever and a seizure due to bacterial meningitis

D. A 3-year-old child with a seizure during fever, with no CNS infection and an identified extracranial infection  
E. A 2-year-old child with recurrent afebrile seizures  

**Correct Answer:** D. A 3-year-old child with a seizure during fever, with no CNS infection and an identified extracranial infection

**Initial Explanation:**

The lecture defines febrile convulsion as a seizure associated with fever in a child aged approximately 6 months to 6 years, without CNS infection. The fever source is usually outside the CNS, such as URTI, UTI, gastroenteritis or pneumonia.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** The child is younger than the age range in the lecture, and meningitis excludes the diagnosis of a simple febrile seizure.
- **B. Incorrect.** A metabolic provocation such as hypoglycaemia is an alternative cause of seizure and should be corrected; it is not classified as a simple febrile seizure.
- **C. Incorrect.** The child is older than the lecture's age range and has a structural intracranial cause.
- **D. Correct.** The age is within the stated range and there is fever without CNS infection.
- **E. Incorrect.** Recurrent unprovoked seizures suggest epilepsy, not febrile convulsion.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Febrile seizure: age 6 months to 6 years in the lecture.
- Fever is present, but CNS infection is absent.
- Common fever sources: URTI, UTI, gastroenteritis and pneumonia.
- A seizure with meningitis/encephalitis must not be labelled a simple febrile seizure.

### Q4. [Moderate] A 2-year-old has a generalized tonic-clonic seizure lasting 7 minutes during a febrile illness. It is the only seizure in 24 hours, and the neurological examination is normal after recovery. How is this event classified?

A. Complex febrile seizure because it occurred with fever  
B. Simple febrile seizure  
C. Afebrile seizure  
D. Status epilepticus  
E. Focal epilepsy  

**Correct Answer:** B. Simple febrile seizure

**Initial Explanation:**

A simple febrile seizure is generalized, lasts less than 15 minutes, occurs only once within 24 hours and is not followed by a focal neurological deficit. This event meets all these criteria.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Fever is part of the definition of a febrile seizure and does not itself make it complex.
- **B. Correct.** The event is generalized, shorter than 15 minutes, single in 24 hours and has no residual focal deficit.
- **C. Incorrect.** The seizure occurred during fever.
- **D. Incorrect.** A 7-minute febrile seizure is not classified as status epilepticus in the lecture's simple/complex distinction; ongoing convulsive seizures beyond 5 minutes nevertheless require emergency treatment.
- **E. Incorrect.** There is no focal onset or evidence of recurrent unprovoked seizures.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Simple febrile seizure: generalized, <15 minutes, one in 24 hours, no postictal focal neurological deficit.
- Simple febrile seizures do not cause brain damage in the otherwise healthy child.
- A simple febrile seizure does not usually require routine EEG or brain CT.
- Treat an ongoing seizure promptly according to the pediatric seizure emergency pathway.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Page 4.

### Q5. [Hard] A 3-year-old develops a focal seizure lasting 18 minutes during a febrile illness and has transient right-sided weakness afterwards. Which classification is most appropriate?

A. Breath-holding spell  
B. Typical absence seizure  
C. Benign Rolandic epilepsy  
D. Complex febrile seizure  
E. Simple febrile seizure  

**Correct Answer:** D. Complex febrile seizure

**Initial Explanation:**

A febrile seizure is complex if it is focal, lasts more than 15 minutes, recurs within 24 hours, or is followed by a postictal neurological deficit. This child has more than one complex feature.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Breath-holding spells are typically triggered by crying or pain and are not epileptic seizures.
- **B. Incorrect.** Absence seizures are brief episodes of impaired awareness without this focal motor/postictal pattern.
- **C. Incorrect.** Rolandic epilepsy causes focal seizures, often during sleep, but the association with fever and prolonged focal event supports complex febrile seizure.
- **D. Correct.** Focality, duration >15 minutes and postictal weakness are features of a complex febrile seizure.
- **E. Incorrect.** The seizure is focal, prolonged and followed by a neurological deficit.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Complex febrile seizure: focal, >15 minutes, recurrent in 24 hours and/or postictal focal deficit.
- Simple febrile seizure: generalized, <15 minutes, single in 24 hours and no focal deficit.
- Complex febrile seizures are associated with a higher subsequent epilepsy risk than simple febrile seizures.
- A focal neurological deficit after a seizure requires careful assessment and follow-up.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Page 4.

### Q6. [Moderate] Which factor is listed as a major predictor of recurrence of febrile seizures?

A. High fever of 40.5°C or above at seizure onset  
B. First febrile seizure after 5 years of age

E. A long febrile illness lasting more than 24 hours before the seizure  

**Correct Answer:** D. Age below 1 year at the first febrile seizure

**Initial Explanation:**

The lecture lists younger age (especially under 1 year), a short duration of illness before the seizure and a lower temperature at the time of seizure among major recurrence risk factors. Family history of febrile seizures/epilepsy and complex features are listed as minor factors.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** The lecture identifies a lower temperature at the time of seizure as increasing recurrence risk.
- **B. Incorrect.** A first event after the usual febrile seizure age range is less typical.
- **C. Incorrect.** A positive family history is a risk factor; absence of family history is not.
- **D. Correct.** Younger age, particularly below 1 year, is a major recurrence risk factor.
- **E. Incorrect.** A seizure occurring early in the febrile illness, before prolonged fever, increases recurrence risk.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Major recurrence risks: age <1 year, seizure early in the febrile illness and lower temperature at the time of seizure.
- Minor risks: family history of febrile seizures/epilepsy, complex seizure, daycare, male sex and lower serum sodium.
- The lecture describes genetic predisposition and rapidly rising temperature as relevant to pathophysiology.
- Do not use recurrence risk factors to diagnose epilepsy.

### Q7. [Moderate] A child is brought to the emergency department while actively convulsing with fever. What is the first priority?

A. Perform lumbar puncture during the convulsion  
B. Give an oral antipyretic while the child is unconscious  
C. Stabilize airway, breathing and circulation, position safely and check glucose  
D. Perform a CT brain scan immediately  
E. Obtain an EEG before giving treatment  

**Correct Answer:** C. Stabilize airway, breathing and circulation, position safely and check glucose

**Initial Explanation:**

An actively convulsing child requires emergency ABC management. The lecture describes placing the child in the lateral position, clearing the airway if required, monitoring, providing oxygen, assessing circulation, obtaining IV access where possible and checking reversible metabolic causes such as hypoglycaemia and hypocalcaemia.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Lumbar puncture is not performed during an unstable active convulsion and is considered only after stabilization when clinically indicated.
- **B. Incorrect.** Oral medication is unsafe in an unconscious or actively convulsing child because of aspiration risk.
- **C. Correct.** ABC stabilization and immediate assessment for reversible causes are the priorities.
- **D. Incorrect.** CT is not the first step in an actively convulsing child; stabilize first and image only when indicated.
- **E. Incorrect.** EEG must not delay stabilization and seizure termination.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Active seizure: ABC first.
- Place the child laterally when feasible; protect from injury and do not restrain the limbs or put objects in the mouth.
- Give oxygen/monitoring and check bedside glucose.
- Correct hypoglycaemia or hypocalcaemia when present.
- Do not delay seizure termination for investigations.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Page 4.

### Q8. [Hard] A child continues to have a generalized convulsion after initial ABC stabilization. Which drug class is used first to terminate the ongoing seizure?

A. Oral ethosuximide  
B. Benzodiazepine  
C. Oral sodium valproate maintenance therapy  
D. Intramuscular antipsychotic  
E. Oral carbamazepine  

**Correct Answer:** B. Benzodiazepine

**Initial Explanation:**

The lecture lists IV diazepam or lorazepam as initial anticonvulsant treatment for a child with ongoing convulsions. If IV access is unavailable, buccal midazolam or rectal diazepam are alternatives described. Exact dosing and repetition must follow the current emergency protocol.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Ethosuximide is used for typical absence epilepsy, not emergency convulsive seizure termination.
- **B. Correct.** Benzodiazepines are first-line acute treatment for an ongoing convulsive seizure.
- **C. Incorrect.** Valproate may be used for selected epilepsy syndromes as maintenance therapy; it is not the initial route/class described for immediate seizure termination in this pathway.
- **D. Incorrect.** Antipsychotics do not terminate epileptic convulsions and may worsen clinical management.
- **E. Incorrect.** Carbamazepine is used for selected focal epilepsies, not as the first rescue drug for an ongoing generalized convulsion.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- First-line acute therapy for ongoing convulsive seizure: benzodiazepine.
- Routes may include IV/IO lorazepam or diazepam; buccal midazolam or rectal diazepam if vascular access is not available, according to protocol.
- Avoid repeated excessive benzodiazepine doses because of respiratory depression.
- If the seizure persists, escalate promptly using the local pediatric status epilepticus pathway.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Page 4 and Page 12.

## Clinical Case 02 - A child has recurrent unprovoked episodes of abnormal movements

A 7-year-old child has had two unprovoked episodes of abnormal neurological activity separated by several weeks. There is no fever, acute infection, hypoglycaemia or electrolyte disturbance at either presentation. The clinician evaluates the episodes as possible epilepsy.

### Q11. [Easy] Which definition of epilepsy is used in the lecture?

A. Any seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes  
B. A seizure caused by hypoglycaemia  
C. Any brief episode of syncope with limb jerking  
D. Two or more recurrent unprovoked seizures  
E. A single seizure occurring during fever  

**Correct Answer:** D. Two or more recurrent unprovoked seizures

**Initial Explanation:**

The lecture defines epilepsy as recurrent (two or more) unprovoked seizures. A seizure due to an acute provoking cause such as fever, hypoglycaemia or an electrolyte disturbance is not automatically epilepsy.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** A prolonged seizure may meet a status epilepticus definition but does not alone establish epilepsy.
- **B. Incorrect.** Hypoglycaemia is an acute provoking cause and must be corrected.
- **C. Incorrect.** Syncope with brief jerking can mimic a seizure but is not necessarily epileptic.
- **D. Correct.** This is the definition stated in the source.
- **E. Incorrect.** A seizure associated with fever in the appropriate age range and without CNS infection is a febrile seizure, not epilepsy.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Epilepsy: recurrent unprovoked seizures (≥2 in the lecture).
- Separate acute symptomatic seizures from epilepsy.
- Status epilepticus is defined by prolonged seizure activity or repeated seizures without recovery.
- The lecture reports an approximate prevalence of 0.5% in children.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Page 6.

### Q12. [Moderate] A child has continuous generalized convulsive activity for 7 minutes. Which statement best reflects the operational definition of convulsive status epilepticus?

A. It begins only after 30 minutes of convulsions  
B. It is diagnosed only after three separate seizures in one month  
C. It requires a normal level of consciousness between seizures  
D. It begins when a convulsive seizure lasts 5 minutes or more, requiring emergency treatment  
E. It is defined by any seizure lasting more than 15 seconds  

**Correct Answer:** D. It begins when a convulsive seizure lasts 5 minutes or more, requiring emergency treatment

**Initial Explanation:**

The lecture includes the historical 30-minute definition but also notes the newer operational threshold of 5 minutes or more. Treatment should begin at 5 minutes because a seizure continuing beyond this point is less likely to stop spontaneously and requires emergency management.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Thirty minutes is the older definition; it is unsafe to wait this long before treatment.
- **B. Incorrect.** Status epilepticus is defined by duration or recurrent seizures without recovery, not by monthly frequency.
- **C. Incorrect.** Repeated seizures without regaining consciousness can also constitute status epilepticus.
- **D. Correct.** The operational treatment threshold is 5 minutes.
- **E. Incorrect.** A brief seizure is not status epilepticus based on duration alone.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Older definition: ≥30 minutes or repeated seizures without recovery over 30 minutes.
- Operational treatment threshold: convulsive seizure ≥5 minutes.
- Treat promptly with ABC support and the local status epilepticus algorithm.
- Refractory status persists despite an adequate benzodiazepine and an appropriate second-line antiseizure medication.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Pages 6 and 11-12.

## Clinical Case 03 - A school-aged child has sudden staring episodes

A 8-year-old child is reported by the teacher to stop speaking abruptly, stare for several seconds and then resume the activity without confusion. The episodes occur many times each day. There is no warning and no postictal sleepiness.

### Q15. [Moderate] Which seizure type is most likely?

A. Generalized tonic-clonic seizure  
B. Typical absence seizure  
C. Focal motor seizure with secondary generalization  
D. Myoclonic seizure  
E. Atonic seizure  

**Correct Answer:** B. Typical absence seizure

**Initial Explanation:**

Typical absence seizures are brief episodes of impaired awareness with abrupt onset and termination, usually lasting 5-30 seconds. They may occur many times a day without an aura or postictal phase.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Tonic-clonic seizures involve loss of consciousness and rhythmic convulsions followed by a postictal phase.
- **B. Correct.** Brief staring, frequent daily episodes and immediate recovery are characteristic.
- **C. Incorrect.** Focal seizures may have an aura, focal manifestations or a longer postictal period.
- **D. Incorrect.** Myoclonic seizures produce brief jerking movements rather than isolated staring.
- **E. Incorrect.** Atonic seizures cause sudden loss of muscle tone and falls/head drops.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Typical absence: sudden brief impairment of awareness, usually 5-30 seconds.
- Abrupt onset and termination; no aura and no postictal phase.
- May occur many times a day and interfere with school performance.
- Development is often otherwise normal.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Page 8.

### Q16. [Hard] Which EEG finding is characteristic of typical childhood absence epilepsy?

A. Burst suppression  
B. Focal temporal slowing without epileptiform discharges  
C. Hypsarrhythmia  
D. Bilaterally synchronous generalized spike-and-wave discharges at approximately 3 Hz

The lecture describes fast generalized spike-and-wave activity of approximately 3-4 Hz, bilaterally synchronous, during typical absence seizures.

- **E. Incorrect.** Centrotemporal spikes are associated with benign Rolandic epilepsy.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Typical absence EEG: generalized 3-Hz spike-and-wave (the lecture gives approximately 3-4 Hz).

- Ethosuximide is listed as the drug of choice for typical absence seizures in the lecture.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Pages 8-9.

## Clinical Case 04 - An infant has clusters of brief flexor spasms after waking

A 7-month-old infant develops repeated brief episodes of neck, trunk and limb flexion, followed by extension/abduction of the arms. The spasms occur in clusters, especially on waking. Parents initially thought the infant had colic. Developmental progress has slowed.

### Q18. [Moderate] Which epilepsy syndrome is most likely?

A. Lennox-Gastaut syndrome  
B. Infantile spasms (West syndrome)  
C. Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy  
D. Childhood absence epilepsy  
E. Benign Rolandic epilepsy  

**Correct Answer:** B. Infantile spasms (West syndrome)

**Initial Explanation:**

Infantile spasms are brief flexor, extensor or mixed contractions of the neck, trunk and limbs, often occurring in clusters around waking or sleep transitions. The lecture places the usual age between 3 and 12 months.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Lennox-Gastaut usually presents later in early childhood with multiple seizure types and developmental impairment.
- **B. Correct.** The age, clustered spasms and developmental concern are typical of West syndrome.
- **C. Incorrect.** Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy typically begins in adolescence, often with morning myoclonic jerks.
- **D. Incorrect.** Childhood absence epilepsy occurs in school-aged children and presents with brief staring spells.
- **E. Incorrect.** Rolandic epilepsy is a childhood focal epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes, often during sleep.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- West syndrome: infantile spasms, usually 3-12 months.
- Spasms may be flexor, extensor or mixed and occur in clusters, often on waking.
- Developmental delay or regression is an important associated feature.
- It may be mistaken for colic or normal infant movements.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Page 8.

### Q19. [Hard] Which EEG pattern is characteristic of infantile spasms?

A. Generalized 3-Hz spike-and-wave discharges  
B. Isolated centrotemporal spikes  
C. A single focal occipital spike  
D. Normal EEG in all affected infants  
E. Hypsarrhythmia: chaotic high-voltage slow waves with multifocal epileptiform activity  

**Correct Answer:** E. Hypsarrhythmia: chaotic high-voltage slow waves with multifocal epileptiform activity

Hypsarrhythmia is the characteristic EEG pattern associated with West syndrome. It consists of a chaotic, high-voltage background with multifocal epileptiform activity.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** This is the typical EEG pattern of childhood absence epilepsy.
- **B. Incorrect.** Centrotemporal spikes suggest benign Rolandic epilepsy.
- **C. Incorrect.** A single focal occipital spike does not describe hypsarrhythmia.
- **D. Incorrect.** EEG is a key investigation and is often markedly abnormal.
- **E. Correct.** Hypsarrhythmia is the classic EEG finding in infantile spasms.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Infantile spasms → hypsarrhythmia on EEG.
- The lecture lists vigabatrin, ACTH and corticosteroids as treatments.
- Tuberous sclerosis is an important associated cause; vigabatrin is especially relevant in TSC-associated infantile spasms.
- Symptomatic infantile spasms often have a poorer developmental prognosis than idiopathic cases.

**Source:** CNS lecture - Page 9.

A. Gabapentin  
B. Vigabatrin  
C. Ethosuximide  
D. Carbamazepine

- **B. Incorrect.** This describes a severe congenital brain malformation, not hydrocephalus.

### Q72. [Moderate] Which sequence correctly describes the normal CSF pathway from the lateral ventricles to the subarachnoid space?

- **D. Incorrect.** This is not the normal ventricular pathway.
- **E. Correct.** This is the normal CSF pathway.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

## Clinical Case 31 - A child has hypopigmented macules, seizures and facial papules

A 4-year-old child has several hypopigmented ash-leaf macules on the trunk and a rough plaque over the lumbar region. There are multiple small reddish papules over the nose and cheeks. The child has a history of infantile spasms and developmental difficulties. The team suspects tuberous sclerosis complex.

### Q177. [Moderate] Which diagnosis is most likely?

A. Tuberous sclerosis complex  
B. Neurofibromatosis type 2  
C. Friedreich ataxia  
D. Sturge-Weber syndrome  
E. Neurofibromatosis type 1  

**Correct Answer:** A. Tuberous sclerosis complex

**Initial Explanation:**

Tuberous sclerosis is an autosomal dominant disorder, with approximately 70% of cases due to new mutations in the lecture. It is associated with ash-leaf hypopigmented macules, facial angiofibromas (adenoma sebaceum), shagreen patches, seizures and involvement of multiple organs.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Correct.** Ash-leaf macules, shagreen patch, facial angiofibromas and infantile spasms strongly suggest TSC.
- **B. Incorrect.** NF2 is associated with bilateral vestibular schwannomas.
- **C. Incorrect.** Friedreich ataxia causes progressive gait/sensory ataxia, areflexia and pes cavus.
- **D. Incorrect.** Sturge-Weber causes a facial port-wine capillary malformation with leptomeningeal involvement.
- **E. Incorrect.** NF1 causes café-au-lait macules, neurofibromas, axillary freckling and Lisch nodules.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Tuberous sclerosis: autosomal dominant; many cases are new mutations.
- Skin: ash-leaf hypopigmented macules, facial angiofibromas and shagreen patch.
- CNS: seizures/infantile spasms, cortical tubers and subependymal lesions; autism/ADHD and intellectual disability may occur.
- Assess renal, cardiac, retinal and pulmonary involvement.

### Q178. [Moderate] Which skin lesion is classically described as an ash-leaf macule in tuberous sclerosis?

A. Café-au-lait brown macule  
B. Port-wine capillary malformation  
C. Blue-grey lumbosacral patch  
D. Violaceous heliotrope rash  
E. Hypopigmented, leaf-shaped macule  

**Correct Answer:** E. Hypopigmented, leaf-shaped macule

**Initial Explanation:**

Ash-leaf spots are depigmented/hypomelanotic macules and are an important cutaneous clue to tuberous sclerosis. The lecture's PDF shows the characteristic hypopigmented lesion.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** Café-au-lait macules are associated with NF1.
- **B. Incorrect.** Port-wine stain is associated with Sturge-Weber syndrome.
- **C. Incorrect.** Congenital dermal melanocytosis is a blue-grey pigmented patch.
- **D. Incorrect.** Heliotrope rash is associated with juvenile dermatomyositis.
- **E. Correct.** Ash-leaf macules are hypopigmented, often leaf-shaped.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- TSC: hypomelanotic/ash-leaf macules.
- Other skin features: facial angiofibromas (adenoma sebaceum) often appear after age 3; shagreen patch over the lumbar region.
- Skin findings may be easier to identify under Wood lamp examination.
- Look for seizures/developmental concerns and systemic hamartomas.

### Q179. [Hard] Which group contains important systemic manifestations of tuberous sclerosis?

A. Cortical tubers/seizures, cardiac rhabdomyomas, renal angiomyolipomas or polycystic kidneys, retinal hamartomas and lung cysts  
B. Myotonia, cataracts and heart block only  
C. Progressive anterior horn cell loss and tongue fasciculations  
D. Recurrent meningococcal sepsis and adrenal haemorrhage  
E. Bilateral vestibular schwannomas and café-au-lait macules only  

**Correct Answer:** A. Cortical tubers/seizures, cardiac rhabdomyomas, renal angiomyolipomas or polycystic kidneys, retinal hamartomas and lung cysts

**Initial Explanation:**

The lecture lists CNS involvement (seizures, infantile spasms, autism/ADHD, intellectual disability, cortical/subependymal lesions), cardiac rhabdomyomas, renal angiomyolipomas/polcystic kidneys, retinal hamartomas and pulmonary cysts.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Correct.** These are the multi-organ manifestations described.
- **B. Incorrect.** These features suggest myotonic dystrophy.
- **C. Incorrect.** This is characteristic of SMA.
- **D. Incorrect.** These suggest meningococcal sepsis/Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome.
- **E. Incorrect.** Bilateral vestibular schwannomas suggest NF2; café-au-lait macules are typical of NF1.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- TSC is a multisystem disorder.
- CNS: cortical tubers/subependymal nodules, seizures, infantile spasms, autism/ADHD and intellectual disability.
- CVS: cardiac rhabdomyoma.
- Renal: angiomyolipoma and polycystic kidneys.
- Eyes: retinal hamartomas; lungs: cysts.

## Clinical Case 32 - A child has a unilateral facial vascular birthmark and seizures

A 2-year-old child has a congenital red-purple vascular stain involving one side of the face, extending over the forehead and upper eyelid. The child has had focal seizures and is being assessed for visual problems. The clinician suspects a neurocutaneous vascular syndrome.

### Q180. [Moderate] Which syndrome is most likely?

A. Neurofibromatosis type 1  
B. Neurofibromatosis type 2  
C. Tuberous sclerosis  
D. Ataxia-telangiectasia  
E. Sturge-Weber syndrome  

**Correct Answer:** E. Sturge-Weber syndrome

**Initial Explanation:**

Sturge-Weber syndrome is a neurocutaneous vascular disorder associated with a facial port-wine capillary malformation, leptomeningeal vascular malformations, seizures and ocular complications such as glaucoma. The matching lecture PDF contains a clinical photograph of a child with a unilateral facial port-wine lesion.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** NF1 is associated with café-au-lait macules, neurofibromas, axillary freckling and optic glioma.
- **B. Incorrect.** NF2 is associated with bilateral vestibular schwannomas.
- **C. Incorrect.** TSC is associated with ash-leaf macules, facial angiofibromas and multisystem hamartomas.
- **D. Incorrect.** Ataxia-telangiectasia causes progressive cerebellar ataxia, telangiectasia and immune deficiency.
- **E. Correct.** A facial port-wine stain with seizures and possible ocular involvement is characteristic.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Sturge-Weber: facial port-wine capillary malformation, often in trigeminal distribution.
- Leptomeningeal angioma can cause seizures, focal neurological deficits/hemiparesis and intracranial calcification.
- Glaucoma is an important ocular complication.
- The diagnosis is clinical and supported by neuroimaging/ophthalmological assessment.

### Q181. [Hard] Which neurological and imaging association is typical of Sturge-Weber syndrome?

A. Anterior horn cell degeneration with tongue fasciculations  
B. Cortical tubers with renal angiomyolipomas  
C. Leptomeningeal angiomatosis with seizures/hemiparesis and tram-track intracranial calcification  
D. Optic glioma with Lisch nodules and axillary freckling  
E. Bilateral vestibular schwannomas with acoustic loss  

**Correct Answer:** C. Leptomeningeal angiomatosis with seizures/hemiparesis and tram-track intracranial calcification

**Initial Explanation:**

Sturge-Weber syndrome involves leptomeningeal vascular malformations that can cause seizures, focal neurological deficits and characteristic intracranial calcifications. Ocular involvement, especially glaucoma, is also important.

**Detailed Explanation:**

- **A. Incorrect.** This is SMA.
- **B. Incorrect.** This is tuberous sclerosis.
- **C. Correct.** This is the classic CNS association.
- **D. Incorrect.** This is NF1.
- **E. Incorrect.** This is NF2.

**HIGH-YIELD POINTS:**

- Sturge-Weber: port-wine stain + leptomeningeal vascular malformation.
- Neurological: focal seizures, hemiparesis and possible developmental problems.
- Imaging may show tram-track cortical calcification.
- Screen for glaucoma and monitor neurological complications.
