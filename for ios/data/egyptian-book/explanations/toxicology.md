## EB-paediatrics-miscellaneous-topics-accepted-Q112 — Q112 — Toxicology / hydrocarbon ingestion

**Source:** Question-bank MD p.161 · printed p.311 · answer-key PDF p.169 · original Q112

**Initial explanation**

Gastric lavage is contraindicated in kerosene ingestion because kerosene is a hydrocarbon with high aspiration potential. Aspiration can cause severe chemical pneumonitis; lavage may increase aspiration risk. More broadly, gastric lavage is not used routinely in modern poisoning care and is considered only in very selected severe poisonings with airway protection and very early presentation.

**Detailed explanation**

Gastric lavage is contraindicated in kerosene ingestion because kerosene is a hydrocarbon with high aspiration potential. Aspiration can cause severe chemical pneumonitis; lavage may increase aspiration risk. More broadly, gastric lavage is not used routinely in modern poisoning care and is considered only in very selected severe poisonings with airway protection and very early presentation. **Why the others are wrong:** (a) Barbiturate: lavage is not routine; any consideration depends on timing, dose and airway protection, but not the specific absolute hydrocarbon risk. (b) Naphthalene: management is toxicant-specific; lavage is not routine and may be harmful. (c) Kerosene: correct; high aspiration risk makes gastric lavage contraindicated. (d) Organophosphorous: lavage is not routine; selected cases require specialist toxicology advice and airway protection.

Curriculum alignment: Rheumatology/metabolic/poisoning 2024: poisoning, first aid and decontamination. **High-yield point / Review Notes:** Do not induce vomiting or perform lavage for kerosene ingestion. Toxicology guidance: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15641639/

High-yield point: Gastric lavage is contraindicated in kerosene ingestion because kerosene is a hydrocarbon with high aspiration potential.

## EB-paediatrics-miscellaneous-topics-accepted-Q159 — Q159 — Toxicology / emergency stabilization

**Source:** Question-bank MD p.165 · printed p.315 · answer-key PDF p.169 · original Q159

**Initial explanation**

In an unconscious child with suspected poisoning, initial management follows resuscitation priorities: assess and secure airway, support breathing and circulation, check bedside glucose, obtain monitoring and IV access, and identify immediately reversible threats. A detailed history, toxicology samples, and decontamination come after stabilization.

**Detailed explanation**

In an unconscious child with suspected poisoning, initial management follows resuscitation priorities: assess and secure airway, support breathing and circulation, check bedside glucose, obtain monitoring and IV access, and identify immediately reversible threats. A detailed history, toxicology samples, and decontamination come after stabilization. **Why the others are wrong:** (a) Establish airway: correct first priority in an unconscious child. (b) Blood for toxicology: may help later but does not precede airway/breathing/circulation. (c) Detailed history: important once immediate threats are addressed. (d) Gastric wash/lavage: not routine and may be dangerous in an unconscious child without a protected airway.

Curriculum alignment: Rheumatology/metabolic/poisoning 2024: poisoning, ABC approach and initial stabilization. **High-yield point / Review Notes:** Do not perform gastric lavage or administer activated charcoal to an unconscious child unless the airway is protected and a toxicology specialist advises it.

High-yield point: In an unconscious child with suspected poisoning, initial management follows resuscitation priorities: assess and secure airway, support breathing and circulation, check bedside glucose, obtain monitoring and IV access, and identify immediately reversible threats.

## EB-paediatrics-miscellaneous-topics-accepted-Q180 — Q180 — Toxicology / cholinergic poisoning

**Source:** Question-bank MD p.166 · printed p.316 · answer-key PDF p.169 · original Q180

**Initial explanation**

Muscarine stimulates muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, producing a cholinergic syndrome (salivation, lacrimation, urination, diarrhoea, gastrointestinal cramps, emesis, bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm and bradycardia). Atropine is a competitive muscarinic receptor antagonist and is the specific antidote for clinically significant muscarinic effects.

**Detailed explanation**

Muscarine stimulates muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, producing a cholinergic syndrome (salivation, lacrimation, urination, diarrhoea, gastrointestinal cramps, emesis, bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm and bradycardia). Atropine is a competitive muscarinic receptor antagonist and is the specific antidote for clinically significant muscarinic effects. **Why the others are wrong:** (a) Sodium benzoate: not the antidote. (b) Atropine: correct. (c) Thiocyanate: not an antidote for muscarinic toxicity. (d) BAL: chelator used for selected heavy-metal poisonings, not muscarine.

Curriculum alignment: Rheumatology/metabolic/poisoning 2024: muscarinic toxidrome and antidotes. **High-yield point / Review Notes:** For organophosphate poisoning, atropine treats muscarinic effects, while pralidoxime may be used to reactivate acetylcholinesterase when indicated.

High-yield point: Muscarine stimulates muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, producing a cholinergic syndrome (salivation, lacrimation, urination, diarrhoea, gastrointestinal cramps, emesis, bronchorrhoea, bronchospasm and bradycardia).

## EB-paediatrics-miscellaneous-topics-accepted-Q193 — Q193 — Pharmacology / salicylate toxicity

**Source:** Question-bank MD p.167 · printed p.317 · answer-key PDF p.169 · original Q193

**Initial explanation**

Salicylates stimulate the respiratory centre, causing hyperventilation and early respiratory alkalosis. Toxicity can progress to a mixed respiratory alkalosis and high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. The resulting rapid/deep breathing may be perceived as dyspnoea.

**Detailed explanation**

Salicylates stimulate the respiratory centre, causing hyperventilation and early respiratory alkalosis. Toxicity can progress to a mixed respiratory alkalosis and high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. The resulting rapid/deep breathing may be perceived as dyspnoea. **Why the others are wrong:** (a) Paracetamol: overdose is primarily hepatotoxic and does not characteristically stimulate ventilation in this way. (b) Ampicillin: not a classic cause of respiratory stimulation. (c) Salicylates: correct. (d) Propranolol: overdose causes bradycardia, hypotension, hypoglycaemia and sometimes bronchospasm, not the classic respiratory-centre stimulation.

Curriculum alignment: Rheumatology/metabolic/poisoning 2024: poisoning and acid–base effects; pharmacology. **High-yield point / Review Notes:** Salicylate poisoning can be life-threatening; interpret blood gases and salicylate concentrations with clinical status.

High-yield point: Salicylates stimulate the respiratory centre, causing hyperventilation and early respiratory alkalosis.
