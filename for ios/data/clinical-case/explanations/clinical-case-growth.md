## CC-0015 — Developmental milestones

**Question:** An infant can move his head from side to side while following moving object, can lift
his head from prone position 45 degrees off the examination table, and smiles when
encouraged. he can sit with support The most likely age of this infant is:

**Options:**
- A. 1 month
- B. 5 months
- C. 9 months
- D. 12 months

**Answer:** B — 5 months

Clinical explanation:
The strongest milestone in the stem is sitting with support, which is usually achieved around 4–5 months. Head turning, early head lifting and social smiling are earlier milestones, so the combined profile is most consistent with approximately 5 months.

Options analysis:
A (1 month): too young for the described head control and social smile. B (5 months): best fit and the answer stated in the collected source. C (9 months) and D (12 months): would be expected to show substantially more independent mobility and sitting.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS: Developmental milestones and developmental assessment/red flags.

High-Yield:
Use the earliest and latest milestone clues together; do not date an infant from one isolated skill.

## CC-0016 — Gastro-oesophageal reflux

**Question:** A 7-week old baby is referred with a 2-week history of vomiting. He is being
formula fed (160 ml)every 2-3 hrs. On examination he is well thriving, on the 90"'
percentile and has a normal examination. What is the most likely diagnosis:

**Options:**
- A. Pyloric stenosis
- B. Gastro-oesophageal reflex
- C. Over-feeding
- D. Gastroenteritis

**Answer:** C — Over-feeding

Clinical explanation:
Large frequent feeds in a thriving infant with a normal examination suggest overfeeding/regurgitation rather than obstructive disease or infection. Review feed volume, preparation and feeding technique before investigations.

Options analysis:
A: pyloric stenosis usually causes progressive projectile non-bilious vomiting and poor weight gain. B: reflux remains a differential, but the unusually large frequent feeds make overfeeding more likely. C: correct. D: no diarrhoea or systemic illness is described.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS Growth and Nutrition; infant feeding and reflux overlap.

High-Yield:
A thriving infant with frequent large-volume feeds and uncomplicated regurgitation often needs feeding adjustment, not medication.

## CC-0017 — Malnutrition & feeding

**Question:** A 14 month old infant presents to clinic because of poor weight gain and delayed
walking . History revealed exclusive breast feeding with little baby food. On
examination he has large head, distended abdomen, and palpable swellings at
costochondral junctions.
What is the expected laboratory finding in this infant ?

**Options:**
- A. Low calcium.
- B. Low alkaline phosphatase .
- C. Low phosphorus.
- D. None of the above.

**Answer:** C — Low phosphorus.

Clinical explanation:
Nutritional vitamin D deficiency rickets commonly produces low phosphate, elevated alkaline phosphatase and secondary hyperparathyroidism. Serum calcium may be normal initially and falls in more severe disease.

Options analysis:
A: calcium may be normal or low depending on severity. B: alkaline phosphatase is usually elevated, not low. C: correct. D: incorrect because a typical biochemical abnormality is listed.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS Growth and Nutrition; vitamin D deficiency and rickets.

High-Yield:
Rickets pattern: ALP high, phosphate low, calcium normal/low depending on stage.

## CC-0018 — Malnutrition & feeding

**Question:** A 1 month old infant is breast fed since birth. His weight is 4 kg. The mother is giving
the feeds every 2-3 hours. She is not giving any vitamin or iron supplementation. He
passed 4 yellow stools/day. What should be of most concern about this infant ?

**Options:**
- A. Stool pattern.
- B. Caloric intake.
- C. Iron levels.
- D. None of the above.

**Answer:** D — None of the above.

Clinical explanation:
The described weight and feeding frequency are compatible with adequate feeding, and four yellow stools/day can be normal. Routine iron supplementation is not generally required at 1 month in a healthy term infant; vitamin D supplementation recommendations depend on local policy and breastfeeding guidance, so the source’s “none” answer should be checked against the local course protocol.

Options analysis:
A: stool frequency/colour can be normal. B: weight and feeding pattern do not suggest low intake. C: routine iron is not usually started at this age in a healthy term infant. D: intended answer, with the vitamin D caveat above.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS Growth and Nutrition; breastfeeding and infant supplementation.

High-Yield:
Distinguish iron supplementation from vitamin D supplementation; they have different timing and indications.

## CC-0019 — Malnutrition & feeding

**Question:** A 6 months old infant has eaten a diet with the following content and intake for the
past 5 months: proteins 4% of calories, fat 40% of calories and carbohydrates 56%
of calories. 105 calories per kilogram of body weight per day.
This patient will display symptoms consistent with which of the following?
1. Rickets
2. Marasmus
3. Obesity
4. kwashiorkor

**Model answer:** 4 — Kwashiorkor.

Clinical explanation:
Energy intake is near the stated adequate level, but protein is markedly inadequate. Predominant protein deficiency with relatively preserved calories is the classic nutritional mechanism for kwashiorkor.

Options analysis:
1: requires vitamin D/calcium/phosphate disturbance. 2: marasmus is predominantly severe total energy deficiency. 3: excess energy intake is not described. 4: correct.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS Growth and Nutrition; protein-energy malnutrition.

High-Yield:
Kwashiorkor is classically protein-deficient with oedema; marasmus is severe total calorie deficiency with wasting.

## CC-0020 — Vitamin D toxicity & renal manifestations

**Question:** A 1 year old infant is complaining of delayed sitting and repeated chest infection, on
examination there is prominent costochondral junction, he is exclusively breast fed,
He received multiple injections for treatment of his condition. All of the following
are expected complication for his condition except:
1. Anorexia
2. Vomiting
3. Oliguria
4. Nephrocalcinosis

**Model answer:** 3 — Oliguria (the “except” option in the source explanation).

Clinical explanation:
Excess vitamin D can cause hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria, presenting with anorexia, vomiting, polyuria, dehydration and nephrocalcinosis. Oliguria is not the typical early expected feature; severe renal injury may later reduce urine output.

Options analysis:
1 and 2: common hypercalcaemia symptoms. 3: intended “except” answer, though severe renal injury can eventually cause oliguria. 4: recognized complication of hypercalciuria.

Curriculum alignment:
SHETS Growth and Nutrition/Nephrology; vitamin D toxicity and renal complications.

High-Yield:
Vitamin D toxicity causes hypercalcaemia and hypercalciuria; monitor calcium and renal function.

is being formula fed (160 ml)every 2-3 hrs. On examination he is
well.thriving, on the 90"' percentile and has a normal examination.
What is the most likely diagnosis:

**Options:**
- A. Pyloric stenosis
- B. Gastro-oesophageal reflex
- C. Over-feeding
- D. gastroenteritis

**Answer:** C — Over-feeding

Clinical explanation:
The infant is thriving at the 90th percentile with a normal examination despite frequent large formula feeds. A volume of 160 mL every 2–3 hours is excessive for many 7-week-old infants and can cause regurgitation/vomiting. Review total daily volume and feeding technique.

high normal. If  it  was pyloric stenosis, there should be vomiting and 
malnutrition 
⇒ Choice (B): occurs on the second week, the child would not be on the 90th 
percentile, would not be able to eat every 2 to 3 hours. 
⇒ Choice (C): points with: high dose formula, high amount (every 2-3 hours). 
⇒ Choice (D): there are no inflammatory symptoms or signs, no diarrhea.

Options analysis:
A — pyloric stenosis usually causes progressive forceful non-bilious vomiting and impaired weight gain/dehydration; B — reflux is possible but does not best explain excessive intake with thriving; C — correct; D — gastroenteritis would usually have acute systemic or diarrheal features.
